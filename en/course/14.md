## 14.1 Why probabilistic forecasting

Almost all the models so far output a **point forecast** $\hat{x}_t$ — "100 units tomorrow". But when it is time to make a decision, what you really want to know is "could it fall below 80? How high could it go?" **A point forecast throws away the uncertainty**, and the uncertainty is precisely what matters for decisions:

- **Inventory**: stock should be planned at a "high quantile" to leave a safety margin, since stock-outs cost more than overstock.
- **Power grids**: capacity must be planned for extreme peaks, not just the mean.
- **Financial risk**: the tails are the whole point — how much do you lose in the worst 5% of cases (VaR)?

The motivation for probabilistic forecasting is thus: **output a distribution, not a point**, and hand "how certain" over to the downstream decision as well.

## 14.2 Three ways to express uncertainty

| Form | Output | Example |
|------|------|------|
| **Prediction interval** | Lower and upper bounds | 90% interval [80, 120] |
| **Quantile forecast** | Several quantiles | Forecast the 10% / 50% / 90% quantiles |
| **Full distribution** | A parametric distribution | Output a Gaussian's $\mu,\sigma$; negative binomial for counts |

From coarse to fine: intervals are the most intuitive, quantiles more flexible, and a full distribution carries the most information. Models such as DeepAR, N-BEATS and Chronos support probabilistic outputs natively.

## 14.3 Quantiles and the pinball loss

The most common and most practical approach is to forecast **quantiles** directly. The training objective is the **pinball (quantile) loss** — for the $\tau$-th quantile, it penalizes "forecasting too low" and "forecasting too high" asymmetrically:

$$L_\tau(y, \hat{y}) = \begin{cases} \tau\,(y-\hat{y}), & y \ge \hat{y}\\ (1-\tau)\,(\hat{y}-y), & y < \hat{y} \end{cases}$$

Intuition: when forecasting the 90th percentile ($\tau=0.9$), underestimating is penalized 9 times more than overestimating — forcing the model to raise this line until "90% of the true values fall below it". Train one line for each $\tau$ you want and you get a set of quantile forecasts.

## 14.4 How to evaluate probabilistic forecasts

The MAE / RMSE of point forecasts no longer suffice here; dedicated scores are needed:

- **Pinball loss**: also used for evaluation, as a weighted average over quantiles.
- **CRPS (continuous ranked probability score)**: measures how well the **whole forecast distribution** fits the true value; it is the "general-purpose RMSE" of probabilistic forecasting. The more concentrated the distribution and the better centred on the true value, the smaller the CRPS.
- **Coverage test**: does the claimed 90% interval really contain about 90% of the true values? A poorly calibrated interval "claims 90 but actually covers only 70".

## 14.5 Two ways to produce a distribution

- **Parametric**: the model directly outputs the parameters of a distribution (e.g. a Gaussian's $\mu,\sigma$) — simple, but it assumes the shape of the distribution.
- **Non-parametric / sampling**: use Monte Carlo dropout, deep ensembles, or bootstrap resampling of residuals to **sample many future trajectories**, then read the quantiles off the samples. No shape is assumed, so it is more flexible.

## 14.6 Practical tips

- **Calibrate before you talk about accuracy**: a model whose "intervals are too narrow and miss their coverage target" gives a false sense of security, which is more dangerous than a point forecast.
- **Look at the tails separately**: risk scenarios care about extreme quantiles (99%, 1%), which are precisely the hardest to estimate — combine with the log / heavy-tail handling of lecture 12.
- **Choose metrics by the decision**: if stock-outs are expensive, focus on optimizing the high quantiles; if overall calibration matters, look at CRPS.
