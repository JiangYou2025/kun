> This is one of the [paradoxes of time](../), and the closing story of the series.

The problem was devised by the physicist William Newcomb and published by the philosopher Robert Nozick in 1969. It immediately became a touchstone of decision theory, because two highly plausible principles give **completely opposite** answers.

The difficulty is precisely a collision between two great principles of decision:

- **The dominance principle (take both)**: the money in B is already set and will not change because of what you choose now. Whether or not B contains money, “take both” earns $1,000 more than “take only B”. So a rational person should take everything.
- **Expected utility / the evidential principle (take only B)**: the predictor is almost never wrong. Nearly everyone who took only B got $1 million, and nearly everyone who took both got only $1,000. Given that track record, you should take only B.

Philosophers still have no consensus — the problem cleanly splits the two major decision theories. **Causal decision theory (CDT)** says “take both”: your choice cannot reach back and affect boxes whose contents are already settled, so correlation is beside the point. **Evidential decision theory (EDT)** says “take only B”: your choice is the best evidence of how much money the predictor put in, and you should act on the good news it brings.

The deeper value of Newcomb's paradox is that it translates the age-old opposition between **free will and determinism** into a multiple-choice question with money on the line: if a predictor really can foresee your decision with high accuracy, then “your choice” and “its prediction of you” are bound together by some common cause — are you choosing freely, or acting out a script that was foreseen long ago? It remains a “stress test” for any decision theory, and it keeps being revived in game theory and AI alignment.
