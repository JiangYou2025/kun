> This is one entry in [the nature of time](../).

At the beginning of the eighteenth century, Newton's absolute space and time were at the height of their prestige. Leibniz would not accept them. In his famous correspondence (1715–16) with Clarke, Newton's spokesman, he mounted a systematic counterattack: an empty, self-subsisting “container” of time is unobservable and also violates the most fundamental principles of his philosophy.

He argued for **relational time**: time is not a container but simply the sum of the **order of succession between events**; without events, there is no time. The argument rests on two principles. The “principle of sufficient reason”: if time were a uniform empty container, why would God have created the universe at this moment rather than five minutes earlier? The question has no answer, which shows that such a time does not exist. The “identity of indiscernibles”: two states that cannot be told apart are one and the same state.

In an age when Newtonian mechanics was triumphant, Leibniz lost the debate. Yet two hundred years later the tide turned: in general relativity spacetime is determined by the relations among matter and events, and Mach's principle, and even contemporary relationalist physics, have all come down on Leibniz's side. What he opposed was not physics, but **a superfluous metaphysical container**.

Relational time is almost exactly the way data sees time: we never measure “time itself”, only **individual events and their order**. Timestamps mean something only because events string them together — with no events to order, the time axis is just an empty line.
