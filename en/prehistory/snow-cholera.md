### Ten days, five hundred lives

At the end of August 1854, cholera suddenly erupted around Broad Street in London's Soho; in just ten days it claimed more than five hundred lives, and the whole neighbourhood was all but deserted. The prevailing "miasma theory" held that cholera spread through putrid air, but the physician John Snow had long suspected that the real culprit was **contaminated drinking water**. The question was: how to prove it?

### The difficulty: finding the culprit in death records scattered across time and space

The difficulty was that what he had was a record of deaths scattered across two dimensions, **time and space** — how many people died each day (a time series) and where each victim lived (a spatial distribution). The curve of daily deaths alone showed only when the epidemic began and when it receded; the list of addresses alone made no sense either. The culprit was hidden at the **intersection** of the two.

### The solution: put the deaths on a map

Snow did something that would go down in history: he **drew each death, by address, as a small black bar on a street map of Soho**. Once the points were marked, the pattern was glaring — the deaths clustered densely around the public water pump on Broad Street and thinned out the farther one went from it. He also chased down the anomalous points: workers at a nearby brewery were almost entirely spared (they drank only beer, not water from the pump); while a woman who had moved far away, but liked the taste of that water so much that she had it brought to her specially, died of cholera all the same. Far from being contradictions, these "outliers" pointed even more firmly at the pump.

With this map, Snow persuaded the parish board to **remove the handle of the Broad Street pump**. The epidemic subsided soon afterwards (though it was already waning by then). Behind him, the General Register Office run by the statistician William Farr was publishing weekly mortality statistics for all of London — and it was this official time series of deaths that provided the data foundation for Snow's investigation.

### What it means for time series today

This was the founding work of **spatio-temporal data analysis** and the birth of epidemiology: only by overlaying a time series (daily/weekly deaths) with a spatial distribution was the cause pinned down. Today's epidemic curves, contact tracing and spatio-temporal transmission models, as well as spatio-temporal forecasting in [medicine and epidemiology](../../applications/health/) and [transport and cities](../../applications/transport/), can all trace their methods back to that pump on Broad Street and that dot map.
