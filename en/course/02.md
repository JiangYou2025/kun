## 2.1 Why start with data

However good the model, it learns nothing if it is fed the wrong data. This is especially true of time series: they have an order, gaps and different sampling frequencies, and **the data-preparation step often affects the final result more than the choice of model**. This lecture is in no hurry to introduce models; it first makes "data" clear — which datasets are commonly used, what format the data comes in, and how to cut one long sequence into individual training samples.

## 2.2 Common public datasets

For learning and for research, people use these public datasets again and again to compare models side by side:

| Dataset | Domain | Size | Features |
|--------|------|------|------|
| ETTh / ETTm | Electricity transformers | 7 variables × 2 years | The most widely used benchmark for long-sequence forecasting |
| Weather | Meteorology | 21 variables × 4 years | Sampled every 10 minutes |
| Traffic | Transportation | 862 sensors × 2 years | Large-scale multivariate |
| Exchange-Rate | Finance | Exchange rates of 8 countries × 26 years | Long-term trends |
| ILI (influenza) | Healthcare | 7 variables × 16 years | Weekly sampling, strong seasonality |
| M4 competition | General | 100,000 series | Univariate, multiple frequencies |

> When you get an unfamiliar dataset, first ask three things: **how dense the sampling is** (seconds / minutes / days), **how long it is** (enough to train on?), and **how many variables** (univariate or multivariate). These determine everything you do afterwards.

## 2.3 What data looks like: three formats

The same time series can be stored in different ways, each suited to different tools:

- **Wide**: one column per variable, one row per time step — the most intuitive, matching a Pandas DataFrame.
- **Long**: three columns `(timestamp, variable, value)` — suited to databases and grouped plots.
- **Tensor**: `(batch, time, feature)` — the shape a model actually consumes.

Formally, a multivariate series of length $T$ with $C$ variables is a matrix

$$X = [x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_T], \qquad x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{C}.$$

Univariate is the special case $C=1$. In every formula that follows, $x_t$ means "the observation at time step $t$".

## 2.4 Cleaning: missing values, outliers and alignment

Real data is almost never clean, so the preparation stage must deal with it first:

- **Missing values**: forward fill (ffill), linear interpolation, or model-based imputation. The key is **never to fill the past with the future**.
- **Outliers**: flag them with Z-score or IQR rules, then decide whether to replace or keep them.
- **Multi-source alignment**: when sources have different sampling frequencies, first **resample** to a common frequency, then **align** by timestamp.

This step has a single goal: turn the series into a clean matrix that is "evenly spaced, gap-free and aligned".

## 2.5 Splitting: train / validation / test

Ordinary machine learning can split data randomly, but time series **must never be shuffled** — otherwise you are "training on the future to predict the past", causing **data leakage**. The correct approach is to split **in time order**:

$$\underbrace{x_1 \ldots x_{t_1}}_{\text{train}}\ \big|\ \underbrace{x_{t_1+1}\ldots x_{t_2}}_{\text{validation}}\ \big|\ \underbrace{x_{t_2+1}\ldots x_{T}}_{\text{test}}$$

Benchmarks such as ETT commonly use 6:2:2 or 7:1:2. **The validation set is only for tuning hyperparameters; the test set is looked at only once, at the very end**.

## 2.6 Normalization: making different scales comparable

Neural networks are sensitive to input scale, so inputs are usually normalized first. The two most common methods:

$$\text{Z-score:}\quad \tilde{x}_t = \frac{x_t - \mu}{\sigma}, \qquad \text{Min-Max:}\quad \tilde{x}_t = \frac{x_t - x_{\min}}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}}.$$

**Key rule**: $\mu, \sigma$ (or $x_{\min}, x_{\max}$) may only be computed from the **training set** and then applied to the validation / test sets — otherwise it is data leakage again. After forecasting, don't forget to **de-normalize** back to the original scale.

## 2.7 Windowing: turning a series into supervised-learning samples

This is the most central step in time series modelling. We cannot feed the whole series to the model directly; instead we use a **sliding window** to cut it into many `(input, target)` pairs: given the past $L$ steps, predict the next $H$ steps.

$$X_i = (x_i, \ldots, x_{i+L-1}) \;\longrightarrow\; y_i = (x_{i+L}, \ldots, x_{i+L+H-1})$$

- **Look-back window $L$**: how much history the model can see; common values are 96, 336, 512.
- **Horizon $H$**: how far ahead to predict; papers usually test fixed horizons of 96 / 192 / 336 / 720 steps.

Each time the window slides one step to the right, we get a new sample — so one long series can generate thousands upon thousands of training samples. **Once you understand windowing, you understand what the input of every later model actually is.**
