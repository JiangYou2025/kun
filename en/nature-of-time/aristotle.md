> This is one of the stories in [the nature of time](../).

In the 4th century BC, Aristotle set out to compile an encyclopedia of nature. His predecessors had spoken of flux or of permanence, but few had tackled head-on the question of what “time” is. In Book IV of the *Physics*, he decided to pull time out of myth and speculation and give it a place where it could be analyzed.

He gave a famous definition: **time is “the number of motion with respect to before and after”**. His method was to anchor time to motion — without change there is no “before” and “after” to count, and therefore no time. Time is not motion itself, but the quantity we **count off** from motion; and counting requires a soul capable of counting to be present.

This definition ruled Western thought for nearly two thousand years, until Newton challenged it head-on with “absolute time”. Its depth lies in binding time firmly to **change**: time is not a self-standing container, but an order that clings to events. This runs in a direct line to Leibniz's later “relational time”.

“Time is the counting of motion” reads almost like an ancient preview of time series: we measure time precisely by **counting events, counting samples**. Every timestamp is a “before and after, counted off” in Aristotle's sense — where there is no change to count, you cannot even draw the horizontal axis.
