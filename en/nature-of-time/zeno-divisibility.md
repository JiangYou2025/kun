In the fifth century BC, Zeno set out to defend his teacher Parmenides: true “being” is one, unchanging and motionless, and motion is only an illusion. To this end he devised a set of paradoxes aimed squarely at the commonsense belief that “time and space can be divided without end”, pushing it into self-contradiction.

His method was **reductio ad absurdum**: assume that time and motion can be divided infinitely, then derive an absurd conclusion. Achilles can never catch the tortoise; the flying arrow is at rest in every “instant” — and if it does not move at any moment, how can the whole flight move? These paradoxes drove the view of time as infinitely divisible into a corner.

For two thousand years people could only answer him conceptually, without any rigorous arithmetic. Only with the calculus of the seventeenth century, and then Cauchy and Weierstrass's rigorous treatment of **limits** in the nineteenth, did humanity learn that infinitely many ever-smaller quantities can add up to a perfectly finite value. Zeno's question unexpectedly gave birth to the most powerful language we have for describing continuity and change.

Today, when we cut continuous time into sampling points for processing, we stand at the other end of Zeno's problem: how do the discrete and the continuous meet? The sampling theorem, limits, integrals — these tools that tamed “infinite division” are all distant descendants of Zeno's challenge.
