In the 1920s, statisticians were facing data that rose and fell from year to year: sunspot numbers, business cycles, agricultural yields. The mainstream approach was **periodogram analysis**: assume the series is a superposition of hidden sine waves and fit it with harmonics. But this "deterministic cycle" view could not explain an awkward phenomenon — the periods drift, the amplitudes change, and clear structure remains in the residuals.

## Key contribution 1: Yule's autoregression (1927)

The sunspot series appears to have a period of about 11 years, yet deterministic sine models fit it poorly, and the residuals are not random. Why is the cycle not "exact"?

Yule proposed an entirely new perspective: the current value is a linear regression on several past values, plus an unpredictable random shock. This is the **AR(p)** model:

$$ X_t = c + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_i X_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t,\qquad \varepsilon_t \sim \mathrm{WN}(0,\sigma^2) $$

Each coefficient $$\phi_i$$ measures the influence of the value at lag $$i$$ on today; $$\varepsilon_t$$ is the "innovation", the random disturbance the system receives that cannot be predicted from history. AR can be seen as a linear system continuously "excited" by random shocks. Take AR(2): the roots of its characteristic equation

$$ 1-\phi_1 z-\phi_2 z^2 = 0 $$

determine its behaviour. When the roots are **complex**, the system shows **damped oscillation** — exactly the mathematical origin of "quasi-periodic" rather than strictly periodic behaviour, which perfectly explains the drift of the sunspot cycle. Stationarity requires all roots to lie **outside** the unit circle.

AR(2) fits the sunspots far better than deterministic harmonic models, and its residuals are much closer to white noise.

By replacing "deterministic cycles" with "stochastic processes", Yule laid down the worldview of modern time series analysis.

## Key contribution 2: the Slutsky effect (1927)

Can purely random input "pretend" to be periodic?

Slutsky showed that taking moving sums (linear filtering) of white noise produces series that look very much like cyclical fluctuations:

$$ X_t = \sum_{j=0}^{q} b_j\, \varepsilon_{t-j} $$

Once a string of independent random shocks is weighted and summed, a smooth, cycle-like appearance emerges — known as the **Slutsky–Yule effect**. It carries a profound warning: the cycles you "see" in data may be nothing more than **artefacts** created by filtering (even by a moving average you applied yourself).

## Key contribution 3: the Yule–Walker equations

To estimate AR coefficients from data, the autocorrelations $$\rho_k$$ satisfy:

$$ \rho_k = \sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_i\, \rho_{k-i},\qquad k\ge 1 $$

This is a system of linear equations linking the "unknown parameters $$\phi$$" to the "autocorrelations $$\rho_k$$ that can be estimated from data"; written in matrix form, it can be solved for $$\phi$$. It is one of the earliest parameter estimation methods, and the core of the correspondence between AR models and autocorrelation structure.

Autoregression, the concept of innovation, and the Yule–Walker equations, together with Slutsky's warning, lead directly to the ARMA/ARIMA framework of the 1970s. From then on, the central task of time series analysis was defined as **identifying the stochastic structure that drives the system**.
