By 1970, the tools — AR, MA, differencing, seasonal adjustment — were all in place, but there was no systematic procedure for "given a real series, which model should I choose, how do I set its orders, and how do I validate it?". At the same time, the spread of computers made iterative maximum likelihood estimation feasible for the first time. George Box and Gwilym Jenkins's *Time Series Analysis: Forecasting and Control* (1970) filled this gap.

## Key contribution: the ARIMA model and the three-stage methodology

Real series are often **non-stationary** (with trend and seasonality), while classical theory holds only for stationary processes. How can they be handled in a unified way, with a workable modelling procedure?

Two steps: first use **differencing** to make the non-stationary series stationary, then model it with ARMA. With the backshift operator $$B$$ (defined by $$BX_t=X_{t-1}$$), ARIMA($$p,d,q$$) can be written compactly as:

$$ \phi(B)\,(1-B)^{d}\,X_t = \theta(B)\,\varepsilon_t $$

where

$$ \phi(B)=1-\phi_1 B-\cdots-\phi_p B^{p},\qquad \theta(B)=1+\theta_1 B+\cdots+\theta_q B^{q} $$

Seasonality is handled by multiplying in seasonal factors, giving SARIMA$$(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)_s$$:

$$ \Phi(B^{s})\,\phi(B)\,(1-B)^{d}(1-B^{s})^{D} X_t = \Theta(B^{s})\,\theta(B)\,\varepsilon_t $$

$$(1-B)^d$$ means "difference $$d$$ times" and specifically removes trend — for example, $$(1-B)X_t=X_t-X_{t-1}$$ removes a linear trend; $$(1-B^s)$$ removes seasonality with period $$s$$. $$\phi(B)$$ is the AR part and $$\theta(B)$$ the MA part. **Stationarity** of the model requires the roots of $$\phi(B)=0$$ to lie outside the unit circle, and **invertibility** requires the roots of $$\theta(B)=0$$ to lie outside the unit circle. The entire structure of the series is compressed into the coefficients of two polynomials.

**The methodology itself is the contribution — a three-stage iterative loop:**

1. **Identification.** Use the shapes of the sample ACF/PACF to determine $$p,d,q$$: the PACF of an AR($$p$$) cuts off after lag $$p$$, and the ACF of an MA($$q$$) cuts off after lag $$q$$.
2. **Estimation.** Fit the coefficients by maximum likelihood or conditional least squares.
3. **Diagnostic checking.** Test whether the residuals are white noise (e.g. with the Ljung–Box test); if not, return to step 1 and adjust the orders.

The Ljung–Box statistic

$$ Q = n(n+2)\sum_{k=1}^{h}\frac{\hat\rho_k^2}{n-k} $$

approximately follows a $$\chi^2$$ distribution: if the residuals still show significant autocorrelation $$\hat\rho_k$$, $$Q$$ is large, meaning the model has not captured everything and needs improvement.

Box–Jenkins delivers excellent accuracy for short- to medium-term univariate forecasting, with complete statistical inference (confidence intervals, tests).

ARIMA became the **de facto standard** for roughly the next thirty years; more importantly, it codified "modelling" itself as a reproducible, teachable scientific procedure.

ARIMA remains a strong baseline against which any new model (including deep learning and KUN) must be compared; its iterative "identify — estimate — diagnose" idea also deeply shaped the later culture of model selection and validation.
