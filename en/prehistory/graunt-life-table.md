### Lists of the dead, kept against the plague

From the 16th century onward, to track the comings and goings of plague, London's parishes published a weekly “Bill of Mortality” recording, week by week, how many people had died and of what — plague, fevers, convulsions, “aged”, childbirth, run over by a cart, every cause imaginable. It was meant as an epidemic bulletin for the authorities and frightened citizens: a rich but “raw” social time series, recorded for some other purpose. Year after year the bills piled up, yet no one had thought about what they might reveal taken together.

### The difficulty: can scattered death counts yield a lifespan?

The difficulty was this: could these scattered death counts — inconsistent in definition and recorded only to report on epidemics — be processed into a quantitative judgement about “**how long people actually live**”? Adding up the weekly deaths was easy, but reading from them “to what age a newborn will probably survive” required crossing a bridge that no one at the time had ever built.

### The solution: turning deaths into computable proportions

In 1662 the London haberdasher John Graunt published *Natural and Political Observations Made upon the Bills of Mortality*. He did something simple yet pioneering: he **aggregated, classified and took proportions of** the many years of the death series. He discarded obviously absurd causes of death, estimated the share of each category of cause, and sharply pointed out systematic biases in the reports (for example, certain diseases being euphemistically recorded as some other cause).

The decisive step was that from all this he constructed the first **life table** in history: starting from 100 newborns, he estimated how many would survive past 6, 16, 26 … and on up to 76. His figures: out of 100, roughly 36 reach age 6, and by their sixties only a handful remain. Crude as it was, this was the first time humanity turned “death” from a string of anecdotes into a **table of survival proportions** that could be consulted and calculated with. The work earned him election to the Royal Society — a haberdasher entering the halls of science on the strength of a single table.

### What it means for time series today

Graunt demonstrated an entirely new path: converting a **social time series** recorded for other purposes into probabilities usable for decisions — leading directly to life insurance, annuity pricing and public health. Half a century later, [Halley pushed the method further](../halley-comet/), using cleaner data to compute an actuarial life table. With this, statistics moved from the stars in the heavens into the births, ageing, sickness and deaths of everyday human life. Today's population projections, actuarial science and epidemic modelling all have their roots in this first table.
