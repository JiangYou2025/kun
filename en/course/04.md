## 4.1 Why plotting matters so much

Time series are one of the few kinds of data where **a single glance reveals a great deal**: trends, seasonality, anomalies and abrupt changes are often more obvious in a plot than in any statistic. Visualization appears twice in the workflow — **before modelling**, plots help you understand how the data should be processed; **after modelling**, plots help you judge whether the forecast is actually any good. This lecture covers both together.

## 4.2 Step one: plot the data

The most basic step, and the one you should never skip:

- **Line plot**: time on the horizontal axis, value on the vertical axis; trend, seasonality and abrupt changes are visible at a glance.
- **Rolling statistics**: slide a window (e.g. 7 days, 30 days) to compute the mean / standard deviation, and see whether the trend is changing and whether the volatility is growing.

```python
df['value'].plot()
df['value'].rolling(30).mean().plot()
```

## 4.3 Distributions and seasonal subplots

- **Histogram / box plot**: check whether the distribution of values is symmetric and whether it has heavy tails.
- **Box plots grouped by month / week (seasonal subseries)**: reveal periodic patterns — electricity use peaks in summer and winter, retail soars in December.

## 4.4 Lag plots: does the series "remember" the past?

A **lag plot** draws $x_t$ against $x_{t-k}$ as a scatter plot:

- If the scatter for $k=1$ is close to a straight line → the series strongly "remembers" the previous step.
- If the scatter for $k=12$ is close to a straight line → the series has a period of 12 steps.

This is the graphical, intuitive version of "autocorrelation" from Lecture 7 — whether things are correlated, check it with your eyes first.

## 4.5 Relationships between multiple variables

When there is more than one variable:

- **Correlation-matrix heatmap**: quickly see which variables move together.
- **Cross-correlation function**: see whether one variable **leads** another by some number of steps (e.g. temperature leading electricity consumption).

## 4.6 After modelling: is the forecast right? Plot it and see

Once the model is trained, the first thing to do is not to look at numbers but to **plot the forecast and the actual values together**:

- **Forecast vs actual curves**: overlaid on one chart — does it follow the trend, does it hit the seasonal peaks and troughs?
- **Residual plot**: plot the error $e_t = x_t - \hat{x}_t$ over time. Ideal residuals should **look like white noise** — jittering randomly around zero, with no structure.

$$e_t = x_t - \hat{x}_t$$

If you can still see a trend or a cycle in the residuals, the model has **missed predictable information** and you should go back and revise it.

## 4.7 How to examine errors

- **Residual histogram**: are the errors symmetric, is there a systematic tendency to overshoot / undershoot (bias)?
- **Error over time / over forecast horizon**: errors usually grow the further ahead you forecast; plotting this shows clearly at what distance the model "breaks down".
- **Overlaying several models for comparison**: plot the baseline's forecast and the new model's together, and which is better is obvious at a glance.

> Numbers (the various metrics covered in Lecture 7) tell you "how far off" you are, but **only plots can tell you "where it goes wrong"**. Use both together.

## 4.8 A plotting checklist

When you get a new series or a set of forecast results, run through this quickly:

1. **Trend**: is it rising or falling overall? Linearly or accelerating?
2. **Seasonality**: is there a fixed period (daily / weekly / yearly)?
3. **Variance**: does the size of the fluctuations change with time or with the level? → You may need a log transform (Lecture 12).
4. **Anomalies and gaps**: are there jumps, breaks, outliers?
5. **Residuals**: do the model residuals look like white noise? Is there any structure left uncaptured?
