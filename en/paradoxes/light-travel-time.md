> This is one of the [paradoxes of time](../).

This is a very real phenomenon in astronomical observation, not a thought experiment. The most dramatic example is the **superluminal motion** of quasar jets: when a blob of matter rushes at nearly the speed of light in a direction tilted slightly towards us, it is “chasing the light it emitted”, so its sideways motion across the sky appears faster than light — as if it violated relativity.

The difficulty is a mismatch between appearance and reality: the “order of events” we see is the order in which the various light rays **arrive**, not the true order in which the events **occurred**. With different path lengths, what comes late and what comes early gets shuffled.

It needs no one to “solve” it, because it was never a true paradox but an **observational illusion**. Subtract the finite speed of light and the geometry, and everything is restored: apparent superluminal motion is just a trick of projection plus time delay, and the true speed never exceeds $c$; and **the causal order of timelike-separated events is preserved for all inertial observers** — no one will ever get the order wrong between a “cause” and an “effect” that are genuinely causally linked. What can be scrambled is only the “visual arrival order” of events that have no causal connection with one another.

Its lesson is simple but important: **“the order in which we see things” is not “the order in which they happen”.** When dealing with any time series that involves propagation delays (astronomy, remote sensing, networks), we must first separate “when the signal arrives” from “when the event happened” — this is the real-world version of **alignment and de-lagging** in time-series analysis.
