### Comets: ill-omened visitors in the sky

At the end of the 17th century, comets were still widely regarded as one-off, ill-omened portents: appearing from nowhere and vanishing without a trace, they streaked across the night sky and disappeared forever, taken as heaven's warnings of disasters on earth. Meanwhile, Newton's law of universal gravitation had just appeared, and it could precisely describe the planets' elliptical orbits around the Sun — but what about comets? No one knew for sure whether they obeyed the same law of gravity. Edmond Halley was a close friend of Newton's, and the man who had persuaded him to publish the *Principia* and paid for it.

### The difficulty: recognizing “the same one” in scattered records

The difficulty was this: historical records of comet sightings were scattered, ancient and imprecise in their coordinates, often no more than “in such-and-such a month of such-and-such a year, a strange star appeared in the sky”. How could one recognize, in this pile of vague old accounts, that certain appearances were in fact **the same** body returning periodically, and then extrapolate the time of its next appearance? That required both a theory of gravitation and the patience to compare centuries of records.

### The solution: historical series + physical law

In his 1705 *Synopsis of the Astronomy of Comets*, Halley worked backwards, using Newtonian gravitation, to the orbits of the comets best documented in history, one by one. He noticed that the three comets of 1531 (recorded by Apian), 1607 (recorded by Kepler) and 1682 (which he had observed himself) had almost exactly coinciding orbits, separated by about **76 years** each. He concluded that these were not three comets at all but a single one, returning periodically along an elongated ellipse — and predicted it would come back **around 1758**. He even foresaw that Jupiter's gravitational perturbation would delay its return slightly.

Halley himself did not live to see the day — he died in 1742, sixteen years before the prediction came true. Later, Clairaut, Lalande and Lepaute spent months including the gravitational perturbations of Jupiter and Saturn, correcting the time of return to early 1759. And the comet duly appeared as promised: on **Christmas night 1758** it was rediscovered by Palitzsch, a German farmer and amateur astronomer; it passed perihelion early the following year, and the prediction was fulfilled. Posterity named it **Halley's comet**.

### What it means for time series today

This is a milestone in long-range extrapolation from **a historical series + physical law**, ultimately confirmed by future fact. It pulled “prediction” out of divination and squarely into science: a **falsifiable** quantitative claim that really was verified. Unlike [the Babylonians, who extrapolated eclipse records purely from cycles](../babylon-diaries/), Halley's prediction had a dynamical model behind it — the embryo of every later forecasting paradigm of “mechanistic model + historical data + extrapolation into the future”: no longer asking only “how did the past repeat itself?” but “given the known laws, what must the future be?”
