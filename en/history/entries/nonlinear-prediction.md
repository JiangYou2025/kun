### Background

In the 1980s, chaos theory made physicists and ecologists realize that a fully deterministic, low-dimensional nonlinear system can produce trajectories that look completely irregular, much like noise. [Takens's embedding theorem (1981)](../takens/) further guaranteed that the phase-space geometry of a system can be reconstructed from the delay coordinates of a single variable alone. A tantalizing question thus emerged: are the irregular series found in nature (fluctuations in species abundance, epidemics, sea-surface temperature) driven by random noise, or are they low-dimensional deterministic chaos in disguise?

### The problem

Traditional tools (such as the power spectrum and autocorrelation) can barely distinguish "low-dimensional chaos" from "high-dimensional random noise": both give broad, continuous spectra. **Is there a workable criterion that separates deterministic chaos from pure noise?** Sugihara and May's insight was that the two differ fundamentally in "predictability": chaos, although sensitive to initial conditions, is still predictable in the short term, and its prediction accuracy **decays systematically** with the prediction horizon; pure noise is unpredictable at every horizon.

### Other approaches of the time (and their limits)

- **Estimating dimensions / Lyapunov exponents (Grassberger–Procaccia and others)**: can characterize the complexity of an attractor, but are extremely sensitive to the amount of data and to noise; short, noisy real series often yield unreliable or even spurious low-dimensional results.
- **Linear stochastic modelling (the ARMA school)**: assumes by default that a series is generated by a linear system plus noise; it systematically misfits genuine nonlinear deterministic structure and cannot answer "is it chaotic?".
- **Spectral analysis**: a broadband spectrum can come from chaos as well as from noise, so it has no discriminating power on its own.

### Method

The core is **local (simplex) prediction using phase-space neighbours**. First embed the series in an $$E$$-dimensional phase space with delay coordinates:

$$ \mathbf{x}_t=\big(x_t,\,x_{t-\tau},\,x_{t-2\tau},\,\dots,\,x_{t-(E-1)\tau}\big) $$

To predict the value of $$\mathbf{x}_t$$ after $$T_p$$ steps, find several of its nearest neighbours in the historical trajectory, and extrapolate with a weighted average of where each of those neighbours went $$T_p$$ steps later:

$$ \hat{x}_{t+T_p}=\sum_{i}w_i\,x_{n_i+T_p},\qquad w_i\propto \exp\!\big(-\lVert \mathbf{x}_t-\mathbf{x}_{n_i}\rVert\big) $$

The key criterion is **the decay curve of predictive skill as a function of the prediction horizon $$T_p$$**: accuracy is measured by the correlation coefficient $$\rho(T_p)$$ between predicted and true values.

- If the series is **low-dimensional chaos**: $$\rho$$ is high at short horizons and **keeps falling** as $$T_p$$ grows; this is the fingerprint of determinism plus sensitivity to initial conditions.
- If the series is **pure (uncorrelated) noise**: $$\rho(T_p)$$ is low and roughly flat at all horizons, with no "good first, then worse" decay.

### Impact

Applied to epidemic data such as measles and influenza, and to sea-surface temperature series, the method successfully identified "seemingly random" fluctuations as the product of low-dimensional nonlinear dynamics, and gave better short-term forecasts than linear models. It turned abstract chaos theory into a tool that can be applied directly to real, finite, noisy series.

### Significance and outlook

This idea of "telling chaos from noise through the decay of prediction" truly connected the geometry of dynamical systems with time series forecasting. It directly gave rise to **convergent cross mapping (CCM)** by Sugihara and colleagues, a nonlinear causal-detection method that infers the direction of causation between variables from observed series. From phase-space neighbours to empirical dynamic modelling (EDM), this line of work remains an active paradigm in ecology and neuroscience for dealing with nonlinear causality and predictability.
