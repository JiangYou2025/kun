After the war, companies needed forecasts for thousands of stock-keeping units (SKUs), and the ARIMA routine of "identify — estimate — diagnose" was too costly in both human effort and machine time to run at all. Operations research needed methods that were extremely simple, could be computed by hand or run cheaply on early computers, and could adaptively track change. Exponential smoothing was born.

## Key contribution 1: simple exponential smoothing, SES (Brown)

For a series with no clear trend or seasonality, how can we forecast with very little computation, being more sensitive to recent data while gradually forgetting the distant past?

Use an exponentially weighted moving average, written as a single recursion ($$\ell_t$$ is the level estimate):

$$ \ell_t = \alpha\, x_t + (1-\alpha)\,\ell_{t-1},\qquad \hat x_{t+1\mid t} = \ell_t $$

Unrolling the recursion is equivalent to weighting the whole history with geometric decay:

$$ \hat x_{t+1} = \alpha\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(1-\alpha)^{j}\, x_{t-j} $$

The weights $$\alpha(1-\alpha)^j$$ **decay geometrically** with $$j$$: the more recent the observation, the larger its weight. The smoothing coefficient $$\alpha\in(0,1)$$ is a memory knob — a large $$\alpha$$ reacts quickly but is easily disturbed by noise, while a small $$\alpha$$ is smooth and robust but lags behind. Best of all is the implementation: only one number, $$\ell_t$$, needs to be stored, and each new point triggers a single $$O(1)$$ update — naturally suited to online use and massive numbers of series.

## Key contribution 2: Holt's linear trend (1957)

SES assumes a constant level, so it lags systematically behind any sustained upward or downward trend.

Holt added a trend state $$b_t$$ and used two recursions to update the level and the trend separately:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ell_t &= \alpha\, x_t + (1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1})\\
b_t &= \beta\,(\ell_t-\ell_{t-1}) + (1-\beta)\,b_{t-1}\\
\hat x_{t+h} &= \ell_t + h\, b_t
\end{aligned}
$$

When estimating the level, the first equation first forms an expectation from "last level + last trend", then corrects it with the new observation; the second smooths "the latest change in the level" into the trend. The forecast $$\ell_t+h b_t$$ then **extrapolates the current trend linearly** for $$h$$ steps. The two coefficients $$\alpha,\beta$$ control how quickly the level and the trend adapt.

Simple, robust and adaptive, it gives a strong baseline with almost no tuning. It was later absorbed into the **ETS state-space framework** (see [Chapter 4](../04/)), and to this day it remains a hard-to-beat benchmark in industry and in forecasting competitions.

Exponential smoothing established a "pragmatic" route running parallel to Box–Jenkins: better a simple model, as long as it is scalable, online and easy to maintain. This philosophy echoed again in the 2020s with the "simple yet strong" DLinear.
