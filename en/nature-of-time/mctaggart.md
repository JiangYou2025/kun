In 1908 the British idealist McTaggart published “The Unreality of Time”. Analytic philosophy was on the rise in the early twentieth century, and people were beginning to examine, under the microscope of logic, the commonsense beliefs that had been taken for granted — and “time really exists” was the most taken-for-granted of them all.

He first separated two ways of talking about time: the **A-series** describes events as “past / present / future”, while the **B-series** uses only unchanging relations of succession such as “earlier than / later than”. He argued that change is the essence of time, and that only the A-series can supply change (the same event is first future, then present, then past). But “past, present and future” are mutually exclusive, and yet the same event possesses all of them — which is **self-contradictory**. Conclusion: time is unreal.

Almost nobody accepts the ultimate conclusion that “time does not exist”, but the paper proved unexpectedly fertile: it forced out the central divide in a century of analytic philosophy of time — **the A-theory (passage is real) vs. the B-theory (passage is an illusion; time is just an ordering of before and after)**. Much of today's philosophy of time is still argued on either side of this rift.

Time series are B-series at heart: we only order things as “earlier than / later than” and have no need for a sliding cursor called “now”. Yet when we forecast or make real-time decisions, the A-series “present moment” stubbornly returns. McTaggart's contradiction lies hidden precisely between “the time of data” and “the time of the living”.
