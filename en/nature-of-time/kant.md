> This is one entry in [the nature of time](../).

Newton said time was absolute and objective; Leibniz said it was merely a relation between events. In 1781, in the *Critique of Pure Reason*, Kant offered a third way, trying both to settle this long-running quarrel and to answer Hume's skepticism about causality and experience.

His method was “transcendental” analysis: asking “how is experience possible?” The conclusion was startling — **time is not a property of the external world but an a priori “form of intuition” of our sensibility**. We cannot imagine an experience that is not in time precisely because time is the **framework the mind lays down in advance** for every sensation. Space is the form of outer sense, time the form of inner sense; they do not come from experience — on the contrary, they are the preconditions for experience to exist at all.

This turned the whole debate on its head: time is neither Newton's objective container nor a pure relation between events, but **an order the subject imposes on the world**. It profoundly shaped the next two centuries of philosophy, and it also planted a question that only modern physics would dare to touch: if time is a form of cognition, how much of it remains in “the world in itself”?

Kant reminds us that putting data on a time axis may not be “reading off” a time the world carries with it, but sorting the world through our own “time glasses”. How much of the axis's uniformity and one-way direction belongs to the world, and how much to us who look at it?
