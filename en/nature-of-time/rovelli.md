> This is the last entry in [the nature of time](../).

In the twenty-first century, theories such as loop quantum gravity have pushed the idea that “time may not be a fundamental quantity” beyond the Wheeler–DeWitt equation into a more systematic picture. The physicist Carlo Rovelli is one of its leading figures; in *The Order of Time* (2017) he explained this cutting-edge idea to everyone.

He strips away our preconceptions about time layer by layer. A universal present? Relativity has ruled it out. A universal direction? Only the statistical tendency of entropy to increase. An independently flowing time variable? The fundamental equations do not need one. His method is to **weave** physics and philosophy together: at the bottom level the world is only a network of relations between events, with no ticking time. We feel time pass because we are macroscopic, thermodynamic creatures who observe the world **in a blurred way**, holding only partial information — and that is why entropy and the distinction between “past and future” appear. **Time is emergent, not fundamental.**

This is one of contemporary physics' most radical, and most candid, answers to “what is time?”: time may not be the underlying fabric of the universe, but a phenomenon that **emerges** from a timeless structure of relations because of our perspective and our ignorance. It carries the whole line of inquiry, from Aristotle to Einstein, to a humble end point — even “time exists” is no longer a given.

After more than two thousand years we return to the starting point, yet changed: time may not be fundamental but emergent. And **time series analysis** lives precisely on this emergent, macroscopic, thermodynamic layer of time — humbly arranging the little data we can grasp along an axis that may not be fundamental at all. That is the echo this collection of stories most wants to leave behind.
