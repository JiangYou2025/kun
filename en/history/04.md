The Apollo Moon landings and missile guidance required estimating a vehicle's position and velocity **in real time** from noisy sensor observations — and the estimation had to be recursive, online and optimal. This was a classic control engineering problem, and its solution in turn thoroughly transformed time series analysis.

## Key contribution 1: the Kalman filter (Kálmán, 1960)

The "state" we really care about (position, velocity) is hidden; all we have are observations contaminated by noise. How can we update our estimate of the state online and optimally with each new observation?

First describe the system in **state space** — a state equation (evolution) and an observation equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{x}_t &= F\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \mathbf{w}_t, & \mathbf{w}_t&\sim\mathcal N(0,Q)\\
\mathbf{z}_t &= H\,\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{v}_t, & \mathbf{v}_t&\sim\mathcal N(0,R)
\end{aligned}
$$

The Kalman filter gives a two-step "predict — update" recursion:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{Predict:} & \hat{\mathbf{x}}_t^{-} &= F\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}, & P_t^{-}&=FP_{t-1}F^\top+Q\\
&\text{Gain:} & K_t &= P_t^{-}H^\top\big(HP_t^{-}H^\top+R\big)^{-1} &&\\
&\text{Update:} & \hat{\mathbf{x}}_t &= \hat{\mathbf{x}}_t^{-}+K_t\big(\mathbf{z}_t-H\hat{\mathbf{x}}_t^{-}\big), & P_t&=(I-K_tH)P_t^{-}
\end{aligned}
$$

At its heart is the **Kalman gain** $$K_t$$, which optimally weighs the "model prediction" against the "new observation": when the observation noise $$R$$ is large (the observation is unreliable), $$K_t\to 0$$ and the filter trusts the model more; when $$R$$ is small, $$K_t$$ grows and it trusts the observation more. The term in parentheses, $$\mathbf z_t-H\hat{\mathbf x}_t^{-}$$, is the **innovation** (the difference between observation and prediction), while $$P_t$$ recursively tracks the uncertainty of the estimate. Under **linear Gaussian** assumptions, this is exactly the Bayes-optimal filter.

ARIMA and exponential smoothing can both be rewritten as special cases of state space, and so both gain **online $$O(1)$$ updates** and **uncertainty quantification**. The Kalman filter is the common foundation of modern ETS, structural time series and Bayesian filtering (particle filters and the like) — an algorithm that came from control theory yet defined half of time series analysis.

## Key contribution 2: the Holt–Winters seasonal method (1960)

Retail and energy data have strong seasonality, and Holt's "level + trend" is not enough.

Winters added a seasonal state $$s_t$$ (period $$m$$; additive seasonality here):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ell_t &= \alpha\,(x_t-s_{t-m}) + (1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1})\\
b_t &= \beta\,(\ell_t-\ell_{t-1}) + (1-\beta)\,b_{t-1}\\
s_t &= \gamma\,(x_t-\ell_t) + (1-\gamma)\,s_{t-m}\\
\hat x_{t+h} &= \ell_t + h\,b_t + s_{t+h-m}
\end{aligned}
$$

The three recursions roll forward the level, the trend and the seasonal indices respectively; the seasonal term $$s_t$$ is refreshed at the same phase only once per period $$m$$. A forecast adds the three together, using for the seasonal term "the corresponding phase one period earlier". $$\gamma$$ controls how quickly the seasonal pattern adapts.

It became the workhorse baseline for seasonal forecasting and, together with Holt's method, was unified into the ETS framework.

State space plus recursive filtering injected control theory's "online, optimal, uncertainty-aware" genes into time series analysis, an influence that continues in today's probabilistic forecasting and Bayesian methods.
