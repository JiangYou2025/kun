## 17.1 Motivation: from "forecasting a line" to "generating many futures"

So far, most models have output a single, deterministic forecast curve. But the future is inherently **not unique** — as lecture 14 already said, what we want is a distribution. **Generative models** take this to the extreme: instead of forecasting one line, they learn to **sample many realistic future trajectories**, each one a possible course of events. Pool these samples and you naturally obtain prediction intervals, quantiles, and even the full distribution. Diffusion and flow matching are currently the strongest family of generative methods, and they have made their way from images and video into time series.

## 17.2 Diffusion models: add noise, then learn to denoise

The idea behind **diffusion models (DDPM)** is surprisingly elegant, and runs in two directions:

- **Forward process**: add **Gaussian noise to the real data step by step** until it becomes pure noise. This step is fixed and needs no learning.

$$x_k = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_k}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_k}\,\epsilon,\qquad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I).$$

- **Reverse process**: train a network to **predict and remove this noise**, recovering a real sample from pure noise step by step.

The training objective is as simple as having the network guess the noise $\epsilon$ added at each step. At generation time, you start from random noise and denoise repeatedly, and a realistic series "grows" out of it.

## 17.3 Flow matching: straightening denoising into a single path

**Flow matching (2023)** is a newer, more efficient family of methods. Rather than adding noise step by step, it directly learns a **velocity field** that transports a simple distribution (noise) continuously to the data distribution along as "straight" a path as possible:

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = v_\theta(x, t).$$

During training, the network is made to match the velocity along a preset probability path (commonly the straight-line interpolation $x_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1$). The benefits are **straighter paths, fewer sampling steps and more stable training** — much faster than diffusion at the same quality, and the mainstream direction around 2025.

## 17.4 Using it for forecasting: conditional generation

Forecasting needs "**given the history**, generate the future", so we use **conditional generation**: past observations are fed in as a condition $c$, and the model learns the conditional distribution $p(\text{future}\mid c)$. Representative work:

- **TimeGrad (2021)**: autoregressive diffusion, generating the multivariate future step by step.
- **CSDI (2021)**: conditional diffusion, strong at **imputation** and forecasting.
- **Flow-matching forecasters (2024–2025)**: replace diffusion with flow matching for a large speed-up.

Sample dozens or hundreds of trajectories, then take quantiles / the mean, and you get both a point forecast and its uncertainty at once.

## 17.5 Use cases

- **Probabilistic forecasting**: they output distributions by nature, connecting seamlessly with lecture 14, and are especially suited to risk decisions that need the full distribution.
- **Missing-value imputation**: treat the "missing" part as what is to be generated, conditioned on the known values — exactly what CSDI does.
- **Synthetic data**: generate realistic time series for data augmentation, privacy protection and stress testing.
- **Multivariate / spatio-temporal fields**: for high-dimensional fields such as weather and traffic, generative models can sample coherent futures of the entire field.

## 17.6 Practical tips

- **Slow, but getting faster**: diffusion sampling takes dozens to hundreds of steps and is on the slow side; flow matching and various distillation / few-step sampling techniques are rapidly closing the gap.
- **Ask whether it's worth it first**: if all you need is a point forecast, this is using a sledgehammer to crack a nut; it comes into its own when you need a full distribution, imputation or synthetic data.
- **Still compare against baselines**: however cutting-edge, it cannot escape the iron rule of the whole course — only after comparing with the linear models of lecture 3 and the simple quantile models of lecture 14 do you know what the complexity has bought you.

> Having come this far, the course has travelled from "a string of numbers" all the way to "generating the distribution of the future". But remember the sentence that runs through it all: **there is no best model, only the model best suited to the task, data and constraints at hand — always run a baseline first, then add complexity.**
