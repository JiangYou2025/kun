Probability theory describes **uncertainty** mathematically (the rules are known, we infer the outcomes); statistics works the other way round and **infers the rules from data** (the outcomes are known, we work back to the mechanism). Forecasting is fundamentally about dealing with uncertainty, and these two are the language of the craft.

## Intuition

Probability and statistics are two sides of the same coin:

- **Probability (deduction)**: knowing the coin is fair, we ask "what is the probability of getting 7 heads in 10 tosses?" — from the model to the data.
- **Statistics (induction)**: having seen only 7 heads and 3 tails, we ask in return "is this coin fair, and roughly what is $p$?" — from the data to the model.

[Machine learning](../ml/) uses both ends: we **assume** the data is generated by some probabilistic model (probability), then use the observed data to **estimate** the model's parameters (statistics), and finally use the model to make judgements, with uncertainty, about new situations.

## Mathematical formulation

**Axioms of probability**: $0\le P(A)\le 1$, $P(\Omega)=1$, and the probabilities of mutually exclusive events add up.

**Conditional probability and independence**:

$$
P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)},\qquad A\perp B\iff P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)
$$

The expectation and variance of a **[random variable](../random-variable/)** (describing its "centre" and its "spread"):

$$
\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_i x_i\,p_i,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^2]
$$

**Common distributions**: Bernoulli, binomial, Poisson (discrete); normal $\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)$, uniform, exponential (continuous). The normal distribution is everywhere because of the **central limit theorem** (the sum of many independent factors tends towards a normal distribution).

**Statistical inference**: estimate the population mean with the sample mean $\bar x$, and quantify "how sure we are" with confidence intervals and hypothesis tests.

## A concrete example

Roll a fair six-sided die, and let $X$ be the number shown.

$$
\mathbb{E}[X]=\frac{1+2+3+4+5+6}{6}=3.5
$$
$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{k=1}^{6}(k-3.5)^2\approx 2.92
$$

The expectation 3.5 is the long-run average (even though no single roll can give 3.5), and the variance describes how much the number fluctuates.

```python
import numpy as np
rolls = np.random.randint(1, 7, size=100000)
print(round(rolls.mean(), 3))   # ≈ 3.5   law of large numbers: the sample mean converges to the expectation
print(round(rolls.var(),  3))   # ≈ 2.92
```

This is exactly the **law of large numbers**: the larger the sample, the closer the sample mean gets to the true expectation.

## Why it matters / where it is used

- **Modelling frameworks**: [maximum likelihood](../maximum-likelihood/) and [Bayesian inference](../bayes-theorem/) are both built on probability.
- **[Loss functions](../loss-function/)**: [cross-entropy](../cross-entropy/) and the negative log-likelihood come from the probabilistic viewpoint.
- **Evaluation and [generalization](../generalization/)**: train / test splits, [cross-validation](../cross-validation/), confidence intervals and A/B tests are all statistical methods.

## Relevance to time series forecasting

- A time series can be viewed as a sequence of [random variables](../random-variable/) evolving over time; stationarity, autocorrelation and noise distributions are all expressed in the language of probability.
- **Probabilistic forecasting** gives not just a point estimate but a distribution or an interval — answering "how likely is the future value to fall within a given range", which is crucial for risk-based decisions.
- When evaluating the forecasts of KUN (Kernel U-Net), only statistical metrics (RMSE, MAE, coverage) combined with uncertainty quantification can tell whether the model is truly reliable.

## Summary

- Probability: from model to data; statistics: from data to model — forecasting uses both.
- Expectation / variance / conditional probability / common distributions are the basic tools.
- They underpin maximum likelihood, Bayesian methods, cross-entropy and the quantification of forecast uncertainty.
