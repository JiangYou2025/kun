Financial return series have two features that embarrass classical models. First, the mean is almost unpredictable, yet **volatility clusters** — large swings tend to be followed by large swings. Second, many macroeconomic and financial variables each behave like a random walk (non-stationary), and regressing one directly on another produces **spurious regression**. The econometrics of the 1980s tackled exactly these two points, giving rise to two Nobel-level milestones.

## Key contribution 1: ARCH / GARCH — modelling the variance

ARIMA assumes **homoscedastic** errors (constant variance), but volatility in financial data clearly varies over time and clusters. How can the "conditional variance" itself be modelled?

Engle (1982) proposed ARCH: let the conditional variance depend on past squared residuals:

$$ \varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t,\quad z_t\sim\mathcal N(0,1),\qquad \sigma_t^2 = \alpha_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{q}\alpha_i\,\varepsilon_{t-i}^{2} $$

Bollerslev (1986) generalised it to the more parsimonious GARCH(1,1):

$$ \sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^{2} + \beta\,\sigma_{t-1}^{2} $$

$$\sigma_t^2$$ is the conditional variance at time $$t$$. GARCH(1,1) says: today's volatility = a long-run level $$\omega$$ + the size of yesterday's "surprise shock" $$\alpha\varepsilon_{t-1}^2$$ + yesterday's volatility $$\beta\sigma_{t-1}^2$$. One large shock $$\varepsilon_{t-1}^2$$ raises the variance today and for many days after — precisely the mathematical description of **volatility clustering**. Persistence is measured by $$\alpha+\beta$$: the closer it is to 1, the more slowly volatility shocks decay and the longer the memory; $$\alpha+\beta<1$$ guarantees a stationary variance (a finite unconditional variance $$\omega/(1-\alpha-\beta)$$ exists). With only three parameters, it captures long-memory volatility.

GARCH became the standard tool for risk management (VaR calculation), option pricing and volatility forecasting. Engle was awarded the 2003 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for it.

## Key contribution 2: cointegration — long-run equilibrium between non-stationary variables

Directly regressing two variables that are each $$I(1)$$ (stationary only after one difference) often yields a **spurious regression** with a very high $$R^2$$ and no meaning at all. Is there really a relationship between them?

Engle and Granger (1987) introduced **cointegration**: if some linear combination $$y_t-\beta x_t$$ of two $$I(1)$$ variables is instead a stationary $$I(0)$$ series, they are cointegrated, share a long-run equilibrium relationship, and can be written as an **error correction model (ECM)**:

$$ \Delta y_t = \gamma\,(y_{t-1}-\beta x_{t-1}) + \sum_i \delta_i \Delta y_{t-i} + u_t $$

The bracket $$(y_{t-1}-\beta x_{t-1})$$ is last period's "error", the deviation from long-run equilibrium. A coefficient $$\gamma<0$$ represents a **pull-back force**: the larger the deviation, the more this period corrects toward equilibrium, and $$|\gamma|$$ is the speed of correction. Short-run fluctuations (the $$\Delta$$ terms) and the long-run equilibrium (the error correction term) are thus unified in a single equation.

Determining the order of integration requires a unit-root test; the **ADF test** uses the regression:

$$ \Delta y_t = \rho\, y_{t-1} + \sum_i \delta_i \Delta y_{t-i} + u_t $$

It tests the null hypothesis $$\rho=0$$ (a unit root is present; the series is non-stationary). If the null cannot be rejected, the series must be differenced. ADF is a prerequisite step of cointegration analysis.

Cointegration became a cornerstone of macroeconomic and financial econometrics (interest rates, exchange rates, stock pairs) and the theoretical basis of "mean-reversion" strategies in statistical arbitrage. Granger shared the Nobel Prize the same year.

The 1980s extended time series analysis from "modelling the mean" to "modelling the variance" and "modelling long-run multivariate relationships", greatly widening its range of applications and, above all, laying the foundations of modern financial econometrics.
