> This is one of the [paradoxes of time](../).

Special relativity (1905) brought us “moving clocks run slow”: a clock moving fast relative to you ticks more slowly than yours. The twin scenario pushes this to the extreme and creates an apparent symmetric deadlock — if who is moving and who is at rest is just a matter of point of view, each twin should find the other younger, yet when they meet again their ages can have only one answer.

The difficulty is precisely this **symmetry**: if the two twins were completely equivalent, the conclusion would contradict itself.

Many people meeting it for the first time take it as a flaw in relativity. But the symmetry is false: the twin who stays home remains in a single inertial frame the whole time, while the traveller must **accelerate, turn around, and decelerate** to come back — they go through a non-inertial phase of acceleration, so the two situations are not equivalent.

Resolving it needs no new theory, only a careful accounting of spacetime geometry. In Minkowski spacetime, the proper time each person experiences is the “length” of their worldline

$$\tau=\int\sqrt{1-\frac{v^2}{c^2}}\;dt,$$

and the stay-at-home twin, who travels a “straight line” (always inertial), accumulates the longest proper time; the traveller, who follows a bent path, accumulates less. So **the traveller really is younger. This is not a contradiction but a geometric fact of spacetime: “between two points, the straight line is the longest”.**

In 1971 the Hafele–Keating experiment confirmed it directly with atomic clocks flown around the Earth. The word *paradox* here is a reminder that in relativity, “symmetry” must take the whole history of acceleration into account: over the same stretch of time, different worldlines really do let different amounts of time pass.
