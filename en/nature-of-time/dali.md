> This is one of the stories in [the nature of time](../).

In 1931, Dalí painted *The Persistence of Memory*. Surrealism was at its height; inspired by Freud's theory of the unconscious, artists were eager to bring dreams, memory and the irrational onto the canvas. And Einstein's relativity had just overturned “absolute time”, so the public dimly sensed that time was no longer hard.

In the painting, several pocket watches droop like melting cheese, with ants and a fly crawling over them — hard, precise timekeepers painted as soft, warped, rotting things. Dalí's “method” is visual **irony**: using the very object that ought to be most precise to deny precision. By his own mocking account, the inspiration came from a Camembert melting in the sun. Whether or not it alludes to relativity, the intuition that “time can soften, bend and rot” hits you at once.

The painting became the most famous image of time of the 20th century, a manifesto with no need for words: **the objective time of clocks is an illusion; real time is subjective, malleable, and twisted by memory and dreams.** It condensed Bergson's and Proust's reflections on “lived time” into a picture no one forgets once they have seen it.

The melting clock is the most elegant protest against “treating time as a rigid scale”. While we line data up along an even horizontal axis, Dalí murmurs a reminder at our side: the time you are measuring may be far less hard than it looks.
