> This is one of the stories in [the nature of time](../).

At the end of the 4th century AD, Augustine interrogated time in Book XI of the *Confessions*. Christian theology was then merging with Greek philosophy, and a pointed question lay before him: what was God doing “before creation”? To answer it, one first had to understand time itself. He left a line that has echoed through the ages: “What then is time? If no one asks me, I know; if I wish to explain it to one who asks, I know not.”

His method was **introspection**. Take time apart: the past no longer exists, the future does not yet exist, and the present is so brief it has no extension — all three “are not”, and yet time plainly passes. His way out was to place time inside the **mind**: the past is the present “memory”, the future the present “expectation”, the present the present “attention”. Time is not an external ruler but a **distension of the mind (distentio animi)**.

This was the first time in the West that time was thoroughly **subjectivized** and made psychological — fifteen hundred years before Bergson's “duration” and Husserl's consciousness of internal time. It stood as a rival to Aristotle's “objective counting” of time and opened the long side branch of “time is something experienced”.

Augustine put his finger on a crack we still cannot get around: the “time” measured by clocks and the time we **experience** have never been the same thing. Time series deal with the former; yet whom the data are measured for, and who interprets them, always comes back to the latter — to a mind that remembers and expects.
