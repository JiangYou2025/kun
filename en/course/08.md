## 8.1 Why learn the classical models first

Before deep learning, statisticians spent decades refining a set of simple, interpretable methods — the ARIMA family. It remains to this day **a strong baseline for univariate, short- to medium-length series forecasting**: few parameters, fast training, a clear meaning for every coefficient, and when data is scarce it often beats fancy neural networks. The stationarity (Lecture 6) and autocorrelation (Lecture 7) of the previous lectures were paving the way for this one — now we assemble those tools into complete models.

## 8.2 AR: the future is the past values

**Autoregression AR(p)**: predict the next step with a linear combination of the past $p$ steps.

$$x_t = c + \phi_1 x_{t-1} + \phi_2 x_{t-2} + \cdots + \phi_p x_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t.$$

This is the formal version of the linear model from Lecture 3. The core idea: **the future of a series is a function of its own past**. The parameters $\phi$ are solved by least squares or the Yule-Walker equations. $\phi_1$ close to 1 indicates strong inertia; close to 0, a quick return to the mean.

## 8.3 MA: the future is the past "surprises"

**Moving average MA(q)**: a linear combination of the past $q$ steps' **forecast errors**.

$$x_t = c + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1 \varepsilon_{t-1} + \cdots + \theta_q \varepsilon_{t-q}.$$

An intuitive contrast: AR says "the future is determined by past **values**" (inertia, reversion to the mean); MA says "the future is determined by past **shocks / errors**" (the aftermath of one-off disturbances). The two capture two different forms of a series' memory.

## 8.4 ARIMA: differencing + AR + MA

Real series are often non-stationary, so we difference before modelling. **ARIMA(p, d, q)** = differencing $d$ times (to make the series stationary) + AR(p) + MA(q). The classical **three-step Box-Jenkins method** makes use of exactly the tools of the previous lectures:

1. **Identification**: determine $d$ with a unit-root test (Lecture 6), and read $p$, $q$ from the ACF/PACF (Lecture 7).
2. **Estimation**: estimate the parameters by maximum likelihood.
3. **Diagnosis**: check whether the residuals are white noise (Ljung-Box from Lecture 7).

```python
from statsmodels.tsa.arima.model import ARIMA
model = ARIMA(train, order=(2, 1, 1)).fit()
forecast = model.forecast(steps=24)
```

## 8.5 SARIMA: adding seasonality

**SARIMA(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)$_m$** adds a seasonal layer on top of ARIMA: $(P,D,Q)$ are the seasonal AR / differencing / MA orders, and $m$ is the seasonal period (12 = yearly, 7 = weekly). It is the "Swiss army knife" of classical forecasting — simple, interpretable, and surprisingly effective on strongly seasonal data.

## 8.6 Auto-ARIMA: automatic order selection

Choosing $p,d,q$ by reading the ACF/PACF by hand is laborious; `auto_arima` from `pmdarima` searches automatically and picks the best combination by **AIC / BIC**:

```python
import pmdarima as pm
model = pm.auto_arima(train, seasonal=True, m=12)
```

## 8.7 Order selection and limits

- **How to determine p, d, q**: first determine $d$ with a unit-root test, then get candidate $p,q$ from the ACF/PACF, and finally choose among the candidates with AIC/BIC — information criteria balance "fitting well" against "not being too complex".
- **The limits of ARIMA**: it is essentially **linear** and **univariate** by default. Strongly nonlinear, multivariate-coupled settings should be left to the deep models that follow (Lectures 9–11); exponential smoothing, the parallel classical route, is covered in Lecture 12.
- **Always report a baseline comparison**: even with SARIMA, compare it against the "seasonal naive method" to prove it really is better.
