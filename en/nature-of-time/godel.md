In 1949, the logician Gödel offered his old friend Einstein a peculiar gift for his seventieth birthday. By then Gödel had already shaken mathematics with his incompleteness theorems, and he turned the same piercing gaze on general relativity to see what kinds of universe its equations really allow.

His method was simply to find a **new solution** of Einstein's equations. He constructed a model of a universe that rotates as a whole and proved that it contains **closed timelike curves (CTCs)** — worldlines that always point toward the “future” and yet eventually loop back to their own starting point. In such a universe, in other words, by following a perfectly legitimate physical path you could return to your own past. For the first time, time travel became a **rigorous mathematical consequence** of relativity rather than science fiction.

Gödel's universe does not match our real universe, which does not rotate and is expanding, but its destructive force lies in the **principle**: general relativity itself does not forbid loops in time. From this Gödel drew a startling philosophical conclusion — if time can loop back, then “objective passage” does not hold up, and time may not truly “pass” in the way we think it does.

Gödel turned the hardest of our intuitions, “time always moves forward”, into a proposition that needs extra conditions to guarantee it, rather than one that holds by nature. The one-way “past → future” arrow we take for granted in our data turns out to have no guarantee even from the laws of physics.
