### The best data set before the telescope

The Danish nobleman Tycho Brahe built the finest observatory in all of Europe on the island of Hven. This was before the invention of the telescope: using giant quadrants and the naked eye, he pushed the precision of planetary observations to about **1–2 arcminutes** — close to the limit of what the human eye can resolve. Year after year, for decades, he accumulated an unprecedented, continuous and precise time series of planetary positions, of which the record for Mars was the most complete. When Tycho died in 1601, this priceless data passed into the hands of his assistant, the young Johannes Kepler.

### The difficulty: the stubborn 8 arcminutes

The difficulty was this: from the ancient Greeks to Copernicus, everyone took it for granted that celestial bodies move in "uniform circular motion" — the most perfect and sacred of curves. But when Kepler fitted this model to Mars, no matter how he shifted the centre or layered on eccentrics and equants, one place never matched: between the predicted position and Tycho's observation, the residual stubbornly stayed at about **8 arcminutes** (roughly a quarter of the full Moon's apparent diameter).

How small is 8 arcminutes? With any earlier set of observations, such a discrepancy could simply have been blamed on instrument error and smoothed away, and no one could have objected.

### The solution: let the data overturn the model

Kepler made a judgment that was almost heretical for its time: **the precision of Tycho's data outweighed the authority of every existing theory**. These 8 arcminutes, he said, "could not be ignored", and it was precisely they that "pointed the way to a complete reformation of astronomy". So he went the other way — rather than patching the model to accommodate belief, he let the data overturn the model.

What followed was eight years of gruelling calculation that he himself called his "war with Mars". He abandoned the circle, tried one curve after another, and finally, in his *Astronomia Nova* of 1609, set out the laws of planetary motion: the orbit is an **ellipse** with the Sun at one focus (the first law); the line joining a planet to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal times (the second law). Ten years later he added the third law: the square of the period is proportional to the cube of the orbit's semi-major axis.

### What it means for time series today

This is the model case of "**letting the data defeat the prior model**", and a classic example of distilling laws from a series of observations and then using them to forecast. That 8-arcminute residual was not thrown away as noise; it was pursued in earnest, and it gave birth to the whole of celestial mechanics. It anticipated the central tenet of every later modelling philosophy: **residuals often hide the structure the model is missing** — when the data are trustworthy enough, the little bit that does not fit is not a nuisance to be smoothed over, but the doorway to the next discovery.
