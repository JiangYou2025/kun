### Data used to be tables

In the eighteenth century, economic and trade data existed as tables: one column of years, one column of numbers. To tell whether Britain's imports from and exports to a given country had risen or fallen over the years, or when a deficit had turned into a surplus, readers could only stare at the numbers line by line, silently subtracting and comparing in their heads — laborious and easy to misread. The Scottish engineer and economist William Playfair had worked as a draughtsman at Watt's steam-engine works and was used to expressing changing quantities in drawings. He thought: if mechanical drawings can make dimensions clear at a glance, why not economic data?

### The difficulty: the data have structure, but people cannot see it

The difficulty seems mundane but is fundamental: a long string of numbers clearly contains rises and falls, trends and turning points, yet reading a table line by line makes them very hard to grasp. **The data have structure, but people cannot see it** — the human brain is naturally poor at extracting a "shape" from a column of numbers.

### The solution: put time on the horizontal axis

In his *The Commercial and Political Atlas* of 1786, Playfair offered the answer: draw a time series as a "**curve that changes over time**" — time on the horizontal axis, value on the vertical axis, with adjacent points joined by a line. A famous chart in the book uses two curves to show how England's imports from and exports to a country changed year by year; the gap between the two lines reveals at a glance the direction and size of the trade balance. Trends, cycles, turning points and anomalies leap out immediately, without any calculation. In this book he virtually single-handedly invented the **line chart** and the **bar chart**; a decade and a half later, in another work, he also invented the **pie chart**.

### What it means for time series today

What Playfair did was to **turn visualization itself into a method of analysis**: before computing any statistic, look at the picture, and you can form a first judgment about trends and cycles. Every forecast curve, every trend line and every dashboard today is a direct descendant of that step. He turned "looking at a time series" from reading numbers in a table into reading shapes in a coordinate system — and "plot it first and take a look" remains the first step of any time series analysis to this day.
