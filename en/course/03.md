## 3.1 Why start with linear models

Forecasting sounds mysterious, but the plainest idea fits in one sentence: **the future is some linear combination of the past**. Linear models are simple, can be worked out by hand and are interpretable, and — this is the most underrated point — **on many real benchmarks a single linear layer matches or even beats far more complex deep models** (Lectures 10 and 11 return to this astonishing fact). So we treat it as the starting point of everything and as "the baseline that must be beaten".

This lecture uses only examples and the most basic formulas to take you along the main thread of "fit → forecast → inspect the results".

## 3.2 Fitting a trend with a straight line

The simplest model: assume the value grows linearly with time. Take time $t$ as the independent variable,

$$\hat{x}_t = a + b\,t.$$

$a$ is the intercept (the starting point) and $b$ is the slope (how much it rises per step). **Fitting** means finding the most suitable $a, b$ from historical data; **forecasting** means plugging future values of $t$ into this line.

Example: if a shop's daily sales rise by roughly 2 items a day from a starting point of 100 items, then $\hat{x}_t = 100 + 2t$, and the forecast for day 30 is $100 + 2\times 30 = 160$ items.

## 3.3 What "most suitable" means: least squares

What is "the most suitable line"? We make **the total error between predictions and true values as small as possible**, measured by squared error:

$$\min_{a,b}\ \sum_{t=1}^{T} \big(x_t - (a + b\,t)\big)^2.$$

This is **least squares**. It has a closed-form solution (you can apply the formula directly, without iteration):

$$b = \frac{\sum_t (t-\bar{t})(x_t-\bar{x})}{\sum_t (t-\bar{t})^2}, \qquad a = \bar{x} - b\,\bar{t}.$$

Here $\bar t, \bar x$ are the means of time and of the values. **This "define a loss → minimize it" approach is the common skeleton of every later model (neural networks included)**; only the model becomes more complex and the solution turns into gradient descent.

## 3.4 Using the past to predict the future: the autoregressive view

Using time $t$ directly as the independent variable is too crude — it assumes growth at a constant rate forever. An idea better suited to time series is to **use the values of the previous few steps to predict the next one**. This is **AutoRegression (AR)**:

$$\hat{x}_t = c + \phi_1 x_{t-1} + \phi_2 x_{t-2} + \cdots + \phi_p x_{t-p}.$$

It is still a linear model — only the independent variables have changed from "time" to "past observations". The coefficients $\phi_i$ are again found by least squares. Intuitively, a large $\phi_1$ means "yesterday and today are highly correlated": the series has strong inertia. Lecture 8 extends this into the full ARIMA.

## 3.5 One-step and multi-step forecasting

The model predicts only the next step at a time, so how do we forecast 10 steps ahead? There are two approaches:

- **Recursive**: predict $\hat{x}_{t}$, treat it as the true value to predict $\hat{x}_{t+1}$, and roll forward. Simple, but errors accumulate.
- **Direct**: train a separate model for each horizon, predicting step 1, step 2, … individually. More stable, but you have to train several models.

Understanding this difference matters: **being accurate one step ahead does not mean being accurate many steps ahead**; the further out you forecast, the more uncertain it becomes.

## 3.6 The complete process: a minimal example

Putting the pieces together gives one complete forecast:

1. **Split the data** (Lecture 2): use $x_1\ldots x_{t_1}$ as the training set.
2. **Fit**: solve for $a,b$ (or the AR $\phi$) by least squares on the training set.
3. **Forecast**: compute $\hat{x}_t$ for the times / history of the test segment.
4. **Inspect the results** (Lecture 4): plot predictions and true values together and compute the error.

```python
import numpy as np
t = np.arange(len(train))
b, a = np.polyfit(t, train, deg=1)        # least-squares fit of a straight line
future = np.arange(len(train), len(train)+H)
pred = a + b * future                      # extrapolate to forecast the next H steps
```

## 3.7 The limits of linear models

Linear models pride themselves on being "honestly simple", but you should know what they cannot learn:

- **Nonlinearity**: they cannot capture thresholds, saturation or abrupt changes.
- **Multiplicative seasonality**: when the amplitude grows with the level, a log transform is needed first (Lecture 12).
- **Multivariate coupling**: complex interactions between variables must be left to the machine-learning and deep models that come later.

But remember: **any complex model must first prove that it does better than this line**; otherwise the complexity is wasted.
