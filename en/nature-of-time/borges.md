> This is one of the stories in [the nature of time](../).

In 1941, Borges published the short story “The Garden of Forking Paths”. It was an age shaken by two world wars and by the new physics, and linear, single-threaded narratives of time were beginning to loosen; Borges, with his recurring motifs of libraries, labyrinths and the infinite, turned philosophical puzzles into dizzying stories.

The story contains a strange book that is also a labyrinth: in it, whenever the protagonist faces a choice, he does not pick one path and abandon the others but **chooses all possibilities at once**, so that time keeps forking, giving rise to countless futures that run parallel and cross one another. The method is a pure literary **thought experiment** — using the structure of a novel to give concrete form to the idea that “time is a net that keeps branching, not a line”.

This 1941 story strikingly anticipated the **many-worlds interpretation** of quantum mechanics proposed by Everett in 1957: at every quantum measurement, the universe branches into all possible outcomes. Borges reached through literature, ahead of time, a picture that physics would only later take seriously — time is not a single line but a tree with endless branches.

The essence of forecasting is precisely to face a bundle of forking futures: we assign a probability to each branch instead of insisting on a single outcome. Borges's garden is almost the literary prototype of probabilistic forecasting and scenario simulation — the future was never a line, but a fan spread open all at once.
