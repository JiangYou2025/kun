> This is one of the stories in [the nature of time](../).

In 1916, only a few months after general relativity appeared, Schwarzschild found the first exact solution of Einstein's equations. It describes a body with its mass extremely concentrated, the spacetime around it bent to the limit. At first people thought it was merely a mathematical singularity; no one expected it to point to monsters that really exist in the universe — black holes.

The method was simply to **solve the equations and read the geometry**. The result is dizzying: at the **event horizon**, the roles of time and space are startlingly exchanged. Inside the horizon, “forward in time” and “the spatial direction toward the singularity” become one and the same — someone falling in cannot stop falling, just as we cannot stop time flowing toward tomorrow. Escaping the singularity there is as impossible as “going back to yesterday”.

Black holes push time to the extreme: an outside observer sees the falling clock tick ever more slowly until it “freezes” at the horizon, while the one falling in reaches the end in a finite proper time. In 2019 humanity took the first image of a black hole, and in 2015 detected gravitational waves from merging black holes — geometry that once existed only on paper has become observable reality.

Black holes tell us that the “flow” of time is not a monolithic global background: gravity can distort it to the extreme, even make it trade identities with space. The uniform, universal time on which our data's horizontal axis relies is only a gentle approximation, valid far from strong gravity.
