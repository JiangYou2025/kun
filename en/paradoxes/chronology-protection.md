> This is one of the [paradoxes of time](../).

In 1949 the logician Kurt Gödel offered Einstein, as a gift for his 70th birthday, a solution of Einstein's equations describing a rotating universe. This solution allows **closed timelike curves** — worldlines that bend back on themselves in spacetime: keep heading towards the “future” along one and you arrive back at the very moment you set out, which amounts to returning to the past. For the first time, time travel became something “legal on paper” in general relativity. Later, in 1988, Kip Thorne, Morris and others produced “traversable wormhole” solutions; similar loopholes kept appearing, and the question grew sharp: if the equations do not forbid it, must physics allow it?

The difficulty is that this is a **meta-level** question — not how to resolve a particular paradox, but whether nature has an automatic mechanism that strangles at the root every kind of time travel that would lead to causal disaster.

In 1992 **Stephen Hawking** proposed the **chronology protection conjecture**: the laws of physics “conspire” to prevent closed timelike curves from forming on macroscopic scales. His argument was that when spacetime tries to bend into a time loop, vacuum quantum fluctuations pile up without limit around the loop and the energy density diverges, destroying structures such as wormholes before they can form. He joked that this would “make the universe safe for historians” — **history cannot be rewritten.**

But it remains a **conjecture**, not a theorem — a rigorous proof would require a theory of quantum gravity that we do not yet have.

Its value lies in turning “why can't we travel in time” from a science-fiction question into a **concrete question** about fundamental physics: is causal order actively protected by some law, or is it merely an accident of initial conditions? The answer will have to wait for quantum gravity.
