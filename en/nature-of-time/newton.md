> This is one entry in [the nature of time](../).

In 1687, in the *Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy* (*Principia*), Newton set out to lay the foundations of all of mechanics. To write down the laws of motion he needed a stable stage — a time scale that would not change with any body or any observer. Aristotle had tied time to motion, which was far too soft for Newton's equations.

So he simply **postulated** one: **absolute time**, which “of itself, and from its own nature, flows equably without relation to anything external”. Opposed to it is the “relative time” we measure with clocks. Methodologically this was a physicist's decisiveness — not defining what time is, but assuming an ideal, uniform $t$ so that $F=ma$ and the calculus could be written down. Absolute time was scaffolding erected for mathematical convenience, yet it worked remarkably well.

Absolute time ruled physics for more than two hundred years and held up the whole splendid edifice of classical mechanics. Its price was settled only in 1905: Einstein showed that there is no time that flows identically for everyone. Yet even overthrown, Newton's idealization taught science an attitude — **first give a variable a clean definition, then let the world test it**.

Every timestamp we put on data silently assumes a Newtonian cosmic clock behind it: a uniform, objective horizontal axis shared by everyone. At everyday scales this works wonderfully — just don't forget that this “absolute” axis is itself only an extraordinarily successful approximation.
