## 6.1 Why care about stationarity

Imagine you want to "use historical patterns to predict the future". But if the patterns themselves keep changing — the mean drifting, the variance growing — then "what we learned from the past" cannot be applied to the future. **Stationarity** describes exactly this property: "the patterns do not change over time". Classical models such as ARIMA work well precisely because their input is stationary; so before using them we must first **test** whether a series is stationary, and then find a way to **make it stationary**.

## 6.2 Definition of stationarity

Intuitively, a **stationary series** has statistical properties that do not change over time. Weak stationarity (the version commonly used in practice) requires three conditions:

$$\mathbb{E}[x_t] = \mu\ (\text{constant}), \qquad \text{Var}(x_t) = \sigma^2\ (\text{constant}), \qquad \text{Cov}(x_t, x_{t-k}) = \gamma(k)\ (\text{depends only on } k).$$

That is: **constant mean, constant variance, and an autocorrelation that depends only on the time gap $k$, not on the specific moment**. Most real series do not satisfy this — they have trends (the mean moves), seasonality (the autocorrelation changes with time), and often changing variance as well.

## 6.3 How to tell: unit-root tests

The eye is easily fooled; statistical tests are more reliable:

- **ADF test (Augmented Dickey-Fuller)**: the null hypothesis is "there is a unit root (non-stationary)". **p < 0.05 → reject the null → stationary**.
- **KPSS test**: the null hypothesis is the reverse, "stationary". It complements ADF; looking at both together is more reliable.

```python
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import adfuller
print(adfuller(series)[1])   # p-value < 0.05 → stationary
```

## 6.4 Differencing: removing the trend

The most common way to make a series stationary is **differencing** — subtracting consecutive steps:

$$\nabla x_t = x_t - x_{t-1}.$$

First-order differencing removes a linear trend; for seasonality, use **seasonal differencing**:

$$\nabla_m x_t = x_t - x_{t-m},$$

where $m$ is the period (e.g. the yearly period $m=12$ for monthly data). The **d** in ARIMA(p, **d**, q) is the number of differences; $d=1$ or $2$ is usually enough.

## 6.5 Transformations: stabilizing the variance

When **the variance grows with the level** (larger values fluctuate more, multiplicative seasonality), differencing cannot fix it; you must transform first:

$$\text{Log: }\ y_t = \log x_t, \qquad \text{Box-Cox: }\ y_t = \frac{x_t^{\lambda} - 1}{\lambda}.$$

They **compress large values and flatten the variance**, turning multiplicative structure into additive structure. Data such as finance and social media — "heavy-tailed, spanning several orders of magnitude" — especially need this; Lecture 12 covers it in detail.

## 6.6 Don't forget: normalization and inverse transformation

- Deep models also often apply **normalization** (Z-score / Min-Max), and the statistics may only be computed from the training set (Lecture 2, to prevent data leakage).
- Every transformation (log / Box-Cox / differencing / normalization) must be **invertible**: forecasting is done in the transformed space, and the results must finally be **transformed back to the original scale** to be usable.

## 6.7 Practical tips

- **Why stationarity is a must**: ARIMA assumes its parameters do not change over time; if the series is non-stationary, the estimated parameters are already "out of date".
- **Don't over-difference**: differencing too many times introduces artificial negative autocorrelation and amplifies noise. First look at the autocorrelation plot (Lecture 7) and the unit-root test; if $d=1$ does the job, don't go to $d=2$.
- **Order**: generally, first transform to stabilize the variance, then difference to remove trend / seasonality.
