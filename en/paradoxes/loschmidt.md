> This is one of the [paradoxes of time](../).

In the 1870s, Boltzmann set out to derive the second law of thermodynamics (entropy never decreases) from mechanics, via the motion of molecules. His colleague and intellectual adversary Loschmidt went straight to the heart of it: your microscopic equations are reversible, so how can you derive an irreversible increase of entropy from something reversible?

The difficulty is a profound asymmetry — **reversible micro-world vs irreversible macro-world**.

Boltzmann initially tried to “prove” the H-theorem from pure mechanics plus probability, but the objections of Loschmidt (and later Zermelo's “recurrence paradox”) showed that dynamics alone cannot yield absolute irreversibility.

Answering it takes two remedies. The first is **statistics**: the increase of entropy is not an iron law but an overwhelming probability — high-entropy macrostates correspond to unimaginably many microstates, so the system almost inevitably heads towards them; a cup reassembling itself is not forbidden, just so improbable that you would not see it within the age of the universe. The second, and more fundamental, is a **low-entropy initial condition**: there is a direction because the beginning of the universe (around the Big Bang) was in a special state of extremely low entropy, and the arrow of time points in the direction of increasing entropy. Boltzmann gave

$$S=k_B\ln W,$$

linking entropy to the number of microstates $W$.

**The arrow of time therefore lies not in the equations but in the initial conditions** — this insight is the cornerstone of statistical mechanics, and it remains an open problem in cosmology to this day: why was the entropy at the beginning of the universe so low?
