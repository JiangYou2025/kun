## 1.1 What is a time series

A **time series** is simply a set of data points arranged in time order: $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_T$. Each point can be a scalar (univariate) or a vector (multivariate). The temperatures, stock prices and heart rates you see every day — anything that is "a string of numbers changing over time" — are time series.

## 1.2 Typical applications

- **Finance**: stock prices, exchange rates, volatility → trading signals, risk management
- **Energy**: electricity load, wind speed → grid dispatch
- **Retail**: daily sales → inventory management, promotion planning
- **Healthcare**: ECGs, ICU vital signs → early warning
- **Industry**: equipment sensors → predictive maintenance
- **Meteorology**: temperature, precipitation → weather forecasting

## 1.3 Kinds of tasks

| Task | What it does | Example |
|------|--------|------|
| **Forecasting** | Given the past, predict the future | Tomorrow's temperature, next week's sales |
| **Anomaly detection** | Find abnormal moments | Abnormal signals before a server goes down |
| **Classification** | Decide which class a sequence belongs to | Normal ECG vs arrhythmia |
| **Clustering** | Group similar sequences together | Segmenting user behaviour |
| **Imputation** | Fill in missing time steps | Data lost when a sensor drops out |

This course focuses on **forecasting** — the most central and most common task.

## 1.4 Univariate and multivariate

- **Univariate**: look at a single line. Use the past of $x$ to predict the future of $x$.
- **Multivariate**: look at several lines at once. The variables may influence one another (temperature ↔ humidity, several stocks moving together).

## 1.6 Why time series are "special"

Ordinary machine learning assumes samples are **independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.)**, so they can be shuffled at will. Time series are exactly the opposite:

- **Order matters**: shuffling time destroys information — yesterday affects today.
- **Samples are not independent**: neighbouring observations are highly correlated (this is precisely what "autocorrelation" in Lecture 7 quantifies).
- **Extrapolation, not interpolation**: we predict a **future we have never seen**, and the data distribution may also drift over time.

That is why time series have their own complete toolkit for analysis and validation (time-based splits, backtesting, stationarity…), which this course will unfold step by step.
