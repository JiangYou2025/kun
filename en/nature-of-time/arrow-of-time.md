In the middle and late 19th century, the second law of thermodynamics took the stage: heat flows from hot to cold, and the process is irreversible. Yet at the same time, the equations of Newtonian mechanics — and later of electromagnetism — are almost all symmetric under time reversal ($t\to-t$). A sharp rift opened up: **how can a reversible microscopic world give rise to an irreversible macroscopic one?**

Clausius introduced “entropy” and asserted that it only ever increases; Boltzmann gave it a microscopic explanation through statistical mechanics. He linked entropy to the number of microstates $W$:

$$S=k_B\ln W.$$

A high-entropy macrostate corresponds to an unimaginably large number of microscopic arrangements, so the system almost inevitably heads toward it. **The arrow of time is therefore not an iron rule written into the laws, but an overwhelming tilt of probability** — plus one crucial premise: the universe began in a special state of extremely low entropy.

This gave a profound and modest answer to “why does time have a direction”: the direction is not hidden in the equations but in the **initial conditions**. It unifies the thermodynamic arrow, the psychological arrow (we remember the past, not the future) and the cosmological arrow, and it remains to this day one of the most active questions at the border between physics and cosmology.

Time series take an arrow for granted: the past can predict the future, and the future cannot rewrite the past. This “irreversibility” is the shadow of the entropic arrow cast on data — we can forecast and tell cause from effect only because time has a direction, and that direction is itself, remarkably, a riddle not yet fully solved.
