At the start of the 20th century, physics had an embarrassing crack: mechanics obeyed Galilean relativity, while electromagnetism gave an invariant speed of light $c$. To bridge the contradiction, people imagined an absolute frame of reference called the “ether”. In 1905, the young Einstein at the patent office in Bern decided not to patch things up but to shake the foundations — to shake Newton's great clock of absolute time.

He took just two postulates: the laws of physics are the same in all inertial frames, and the speed of light is the same $c$ for everyone. By logical deduction alone he reached an astonishing conclusion: **simultaneity is relative** — two events that are simultaneous in one frame can happen one after the other in another. Moving clocks run slow:

$$\Delta t' = \frac{\Delta t}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}.$$

Time is no longer a uniform background for the whole universe; it **stretches and shrinks** with the observer's motion.

That was the end of absolute time. General relativity, in 1915, went further: gravity slows time down, and matter curves spacetime. All of this has been verified again and again — GPS satellites must correct for both the special- and the general-relativistic time effects, or their positioning would drift by ten kilometres a day. Philosophically, it handed the dispute between Newton and Leibniz over to experiment for a verdict.

When we timestamp data, we assume there is one shared horizontal axis for the whole world. Relativity says that, strictly speaking, there is not — “simultaneous” depends on your state of motion. At everyday scales this axis works well enough, but its “absolute” status has been taken away by Einstein for good.
