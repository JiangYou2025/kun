In the 2020s, **long-horizon multivariate forecasting** (using a very long history to predict a very long future, with many variables at once) took centre stage. The $$\mathcal O(L^2)$$ cost of Transformers was hard to bear on long sequences; even more surprisingly, researchers found that complex Transformers do **not necessarily** beat extremely simple models on standard benchmarks. The main thread of this decade is a back-and-forth between "efficiency" and "whether complex structure is needed at all".

## 1. Long-horizon Transformers: wrestling with $$\mathcal O(L^2)$$

The quadratic complexity of attention makes long sequences infeasible.

Informer (2021) introduced **ProbSparse attention**, which computes attention only for the few most "active" queries, reducing the complexity to

$$ \mathcal O(L\log L) $$

Autoformer and FEDformer brought in series decomposition and frequency-domain (Fourier) attention, cutting costs further and explicitly modelling seasonality and trend.

The key insight is that the attention matrix is highly sparse — most queries have nearly uniform attention distributions and contribute very little. Keeping only the few dominant queries cuts $$L^2$$ down to $$L\log L$$ with almost no loss of accuracy, making sequences thousands of steps long computable.

## 2. DLinear: the "uprising" sparked by a single linear layer (2022)

Transformers are heavy and slow — are they really better than a one-layer linear model?

First decompose the series into trend and seasonality, then map each directly to the future with **a single linear layer**:

$$ X = X_{\text{trend}} + X_{\text{seasonal}},\qquad \hat X = W_t\, X_{\text{trend}} + W_s\, X_{\text{seasonal}} $$

The weight matrices $$W_t, W_s\in\mathbb R^{H\times L}$$ linearly map a look-back window of length $$L$$ directly to a forecast of length $$H$$ — essentially a "learned weighted average that separates trend and seasonality". It has very few parameters, trains extremely fast, and is **interpretable**: each row of $$W$$ is the set of weights on the historical lags used to predict one future step. Astonishingly, it **outperformed** complex Transformers on several long-horizon benchmarks, directly igniting the great debate over "does long-horizon forecasting need Transformers at all?" and pulling the research trend back toward "simple and strong".

## 3. PatchTST: patching and channel independence (2023)

Attention over individual time points is both redundant and expensive, and the sequence is still too long.

Cut the series into **segments (patches)** treated as tokens, and model each variable **channel-independently**.

With patch length $$P$$, the sequence length drops from $$L$$ to about $$L/P$$, so the attention cost plummets; at the same time, each patch preserves a stretch of local semantics (local shape/trend), which is more meaningful than an isolated point. Channel independence (each variable forecast separately with shared weights) reduces overfitting and improves generalisation — in the same "less is more" spirit as DLinear.

## 4. Kernel U-Net (KUN): unifying "hierarchy" and "flexible kernels"

Transformers can model hierarchy and long dependencies but are expensive; linear models are efficient and flexible but limited in expressive power. Can one framework **have both** — a hierarchical structure, plus the freedom to trade compute against accuracy?

KUN borrows the **U-Net** shape from image segmentation: the series is cut into patches, the **encoder** downsamples layer by layer (fewer, coarser units), and a **symmetric decoder** upsamples layer by layer back to the forecast length. The operation at each node is no longer a fixed convolution but a **pluggable kernel** — linear, MLP or attention, as you choose:

$$ \mathbf{u}^{(l+1)} = \mathcal K_l\big(\mathbf{u}^{(l)}\big),\qquad \mathcal K_l \in \{\text{Linear},\ \text{MLP},\ \text{Attention}\} $$

This layer-by-layer mapping reveals three design points:

- **Hierarchy**: shallow patches are short and capture local high-frequency detail; deep patches are long and capture long-range low-frequency structure. A series rarely lives on a single scale, and the U shape captures several scales at once.
- **Symmetric efficiency**: the decoder mirrors the encoder, restoring resolution layer by layer and avoiding the quadratic blow-up with length of a plain Transformer.
- **Pluggable kernels**: the "compute vs. accuracy" trade-off becomes a matter of "swapping one $$\mathcal K_l$$" — linear kernels give a fast, strong baseline, and attention kernels add capacity when the data calls for it, without rewriting the whole model.

In addition, KUN **outputs the entire horizon in one shot** (direct multi-output), avoiding the error accumulation of recursive forecasting (see the hands-on tutorial in [Using KUN](../../kun/)).

KUN unifies "hierarchical modelling" and "flexible kernel choice" in one symmetric framework, balancing efficiency and accuracy on long-horizon multivariate tasks — an exploration of **structured, efficient design** in the age of large models.

## 5. Time series foundation models

TimesFM, Chronos, Moirai and others are pre-trained on massive collections of series, aiming at general-purpose **zero-shot/few-shot** forecasting and bringing time series into the era of "pre-training + general large models".

In this decade, methods shifted completely from "carefully modelling each individual series" to "large-scale, general-purpose, efficient". The contest between Transformers and linear models reminds us that **the data should decide how much complexity is needed**. KUN stands squarely in this current of thought — using hierarchy and pluggable kernels to find the best balance between efficiency and expressive power.
