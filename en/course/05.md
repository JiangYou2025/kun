## 5.1 Why "take a series apart"

A real series is usually several forces stacked together: rising over the long run, peaking every summer, with the occasional sudden event. If you model the raw series directly, the model has to cope with all these patterns at once, which makes it both hard to learn and hard to interpret. The motivation for **decomposition** is "divide and conquer" — first split the series into a few clearly structured components, understand and handle each separately, then put them back together. It is both a tool for **understanding the data** and a **preprocessing step before modelling**.

## 5.2 The four basic components

We usually view a series as the combination of these components:

- **Trend $T_t$**: the long-term upward or downward direction.
- **Seasonality $S_t$**: a pattern that repeats with a fixed period (weekly, yearly).
- **Cycle**: fluctuations without a fixed period (such as business cycles), often handled together with the trend.
- **Noise / residual $R_t$**: the random, unpredictable part.

An intuitive example: monthly ice-cream sales rise slowly as the city expands (**trend**), peak every summer and fall back in winter (**seasonality**), and a sudden heatwave brings extra sales (close to **noise**). Decomposition separates these three forces.

## 5.3 Additive and multiplicative decomposition

$$x_t = T_t + S_t + R_t \quad\text{(additive)} \qquad\qquad x_t = T_t \times S_t \times R_t \quad\text{(multiplicative)}$$

How to choose? Look at **the amplitude of the seasonal swings**:

- **Additive**: the seasonal amplitude is constant and does not grow with the trend → use additive.
- **Multiplicative**: the larger the value, the larger the seasonal swings (typical of sales and prices) → use multiplicative.

A multiplicative model can be **turned into an additive one by taking logarithms first**: $\log x_t = \log T_t + \log S_t + \log R_t$. This is exactly where the log transform, covered in detail in Lecture 12, comes into its own.

## 5.4 How to decompose: moving averages and STL

- **Moving-average decomposition**: smooth out the trend with a windowed mean, average the detrended series by period to obtain the seasonal component, and what is left is the residual. The idea is plain and easy to understand.
- **STL (Seasonal-Trend decomposition using LOESS)**: more flexible, **allows the seasonal component to change slowly over time**, is more robust to anomalies, and is the workhorse in practice.

```python
from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import STL
stl = STL(series, period=12)   # period must be set correctly
result = stl.fit()
result.plot()                  # three plots: trend / seasonal / residual
```

## 5.5 Detrending and deseasonalizing: paving the way for the next lecture

Why does decomposition matter so much? Because **many classical models require the input to be stationary** (free of trend and seasonality, as the next lecture defines rigorously). Decomposition gives us the means to remove them:

- Detrending → differencing, or subtracting $T_t$ via regression;
- Deseasonalizing → seasonal differencing, or subtracting $S_t$;
- What remains, the "detrended and deseasonalized residual", is what the model really has to learn.

## 5.6 Practical tips

- **Get the period wrong and everything goes wrong**: methods such as STL are extremely sensitive to the period length. If you set the weekly period of daily data, 7, to 30, the resulting components will be completely misleading. First confirm the period with the seasonal subplots of Lecture 4 / the autocorrelation of Lecture 7.
- **Decomposition is not the goal**: it is there to make later modelling easier; don't decompose for the sake of decomposing.
- **Remember it must recombine**: the components must ultimately add / multiply back into the original series, and when forecasting, the forecasts of the individual components must be combined as well.
