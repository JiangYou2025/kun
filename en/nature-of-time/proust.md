> This is one entry in [the nature of time](../).

From 1913 to 1927 Proust published, volume by volume, his monumental *In Search of Lost Time*. It was an age stirred by Bergson's “duration” and Freud's theory of the unconscious, and literature was beginning to turn its gaze from external plot to inner time — how consciousness flows, how memory works.

The novel offers a famous scene: the narrator tastes a madeleine soaked in tea, and his whole childhood in Combray suddenly comes back to life. From this Proust distinguishes two kinds of memory: deliberate “voluntary memory” is pale and dry, while **involuntary memory**, triggered by a whiff of scent or a texture on the tongue, lets the past return with all its texture intact. His method was literature's **microscope** — long, unbroken sentences that reproduce the real, layered flow of time in consciousness.

The work became one of the summits of modernist literature and the most minute exploration of “subjective time”. It confirms and enriches Augustine and Bergson: time is not an evenly swinging pendulum; a chance sensory signal can **fold it in an instant**, pressing together two points decades apart.

Proust reveals a “structure of time” that clocks cannot measure: distant moments can be suddenly linked by resemblance. This echoes **seasonality and autocorrelation** in time series — some moment in the past can always, by “resemblance” rather than “adjacency”, foreshadow and summon up the present.
