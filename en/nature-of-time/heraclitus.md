> This is one entry in [the nature of time](../).

In Ephesus at the end of the sixth century BC, Heraclitus lived in an age that had only just learned to ask “what is the origin of all things?” The Milesian school reduced the world to some underlying substance such as water or air; Heraclitus instead turned his gaze to **change itself**. In his eyes the world was not a heap of static things but an ever-living fire, a continual coming-into-being and passing-away.

He made his point with a saying that has echoed through the ages: **“You cannot step into the same river twice.”** The water flows on; when you step in a second time, the river is new water — and even “you” are no longer the same. His method relied not on argument but on **images**: the river, fire, the alternation of day and night make the insight that “everything flows” (*panta rhei*) directly visible. Change is not appearance; **change is reality**.

This stance set up a landmark for later thinkers and also provoked opponents: Parmenides countered that being is unchanging and change an illusion, and Zeno went further, devising paradoxes to defend immutability. The dispute between flux and permanence became, from then on, one of the main axes of Western metaphysics, extending all the way to Whitehead's “process philosophy”.

Time series analysis is Heraclitean at heart: we accept that data flows and try to model “change” itself — trends, fluctuations, drift. Yet as soon as we say “this is the same series”, we quietly borrow Parmenides' notion of “identity”. Between flowing and not flowing, we have in fact always been walking a tightrope.
