> This is one entry in [the nature of time](../).

In 1895 H. G. Wells published *The Time Machine*. Mathematicians were then eagerly discussing “higher-dimensional space”, and the public was fascinated by the “fourth dimension”. Wells seized on this current of ideas: the novel's Time Traveller opens with a geometry lesson — beyond length, breadth and thickness there is **a fourth dimension: time**.

His “claim” was a bold literary hypothesis: since we can move forward, backward, left and right in three-dimensional space, why not build a machine that moves forward or backward along the fourth dimension, time? His method was to **dramatize** an abstract mathematical conjecture — using a machine of brass and crystal to turn the idea that “time is a dimension” into an adventure one can ride in and live through.

The novel founded the whole narrative genre of “time travel” and profoundly shaped later science fiction. Its most astonishing feature is its **foresight**: only a decade later, Minkowski formally made time the fourth axis of spacetime, and Einstein's relativity turned “time as a dimension” from a literary metaphor into physical reality — although real physics forbids you from going backward at will.

Drawing time as an axis one can move along in both directions is exactly our everyday posture with sequential data: the lookback window “looks into the past”, the forecast horizon “walks into the future”. Wells's metaphor has long since seeped into our tools — except that data can “look” forward but can never really “walk” back.
