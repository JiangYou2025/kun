> This is one of the [paradoxes of time](../).

This is a carefully designed comparison experiment on “length contraction” in special relativity (Einstein, 1905): both sides use the same rules, yet arrive at two opposite conclusions, “it fits” and “it doesn't fit”.

The difficulty is that it pins the contradiction onto what looks like an objective fact — whether the doors closed, whether the pole broke: surely that has a unique answer? Taken at face value, it seems one side must have made a mistake.

To untie this knot, we must first loosen a deep-rooted assumption: we always take “whether two things happen at the same time” to be an objective fact, the same for everyone. Special relativity says precisely that it is not — “simultaneity” changes with the observer's state of motion. The key is this **relativity of simultaneity**: what the person on the ground sees as “both doors closing at the same time” is **not simultaneous at all** in the pole's frame — the rear door closes and reopens first, the front door closes later, so the pole passes through in turn and is never clamped by both doors at once. Both descriptions are correct, because “simultaneous” is itself not absolute.

On **invariant facts** such as whether the pole ultimately crashes, the two frames compute exactly the same result; they differ only on frame-dependent judgements such as “which two events count as simultaneous”.

It turns the relativity of simultaneity from an abstract formula into a picture you can imagine: in relativity, “two events happen at the same time” is not an objective property of the world but a function of the observer's state. What a change of frame can scramble is only “simultaneous or not” — never genuine causation.
