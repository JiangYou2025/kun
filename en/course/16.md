## 16.1 Motivation: where do series come from?

So far we have treated a time series as "a string of numbers" to be modelled statistically. But many series are in fact **driven by physical laws**: weather evolves according to the equations of the atmosphere, a pendulum is governed by the equations of mechanics, an epidemic is pushed along by the dynamics of transmission. The **dynamical system** perspective asks a deeper question — not "roughly what will the next number be?", but "how does the state of the system driving these numbers evolve in time?" Once you understand the law of evolution, forecasting becomes "following the law forward".

## 16.2 State, evolution and attractors

A dynamical system is described by a **state** $s_t$ and an **evolution rule**. In discrete time:

$$s_{t+1} = F(s_t),$$

and in continuous time by a differential equation $\dfrac{ds}{dt} = f(s)$. The time series we observe, $x_t = g(s_t)$, is often only a "projection" of this hidden state.

- **Attractor**: the geometric structure to which the system converges over long-term evolution — a fixed point, a limit cycle (periodic), or a strange attractor (chaos).
- **Phase space**: the space whose coordinates are the state, in which trajectories trace out the system's "motion".

## 16.3 Chaos: the limits of predictability

Some systems (weather is the classic example) are **chaotic**: the rules are completely deterministic, yet they are **extremely sensitive** to initial conditions — a tiny difference at the start leads to a huge difference later. This is the famous "butterfly effect". It is quantified by the **Lyapunov exponent**, which describes how fast neighbouring trajectories separate.

Chaos draws a hard boundary around forecasting: **even if the laws are completely known, long-term prediction is impossible in principle**. This explains why weather can only be forecast a limited number of days ahead — not because the models aren't good enough, but because of the very nature of the system.

## 16.4 Reconstructing the system from one series: Takens embedding

Often we can observe only one variable (say, temperature alone) and cannot see the full state. **Takens' embedding theorem** gives a startling result: using the **lag coordinates** of a single variable,

$$\big(x_t,\ x_{t-\tau},\ x_{t-2\tau},\ \ldots,\ x_{t-(d-1)\tau}\big)$$

one can reconstruct an attractor topologically equivalent to the original system's. This provides a deep theoretical justification for "forecasting from lagged values" (the sliding window used throughout lectures 3 and 13) — the lag window itself turns out to be reconstructing the system's state.

## 16.5 Modern methods: putting physics into the model

- **Neural ODE**: parameterize $f$ with a neural network and learn the continuous evolution $\dfrac{ds}{dt} = f_\theta(s)$ directly; naturally suited to irregular sampling.
- **Physics-informed neural networks (PINN)**: add known differential equations to the loss as constraints, so the model both fits the data and obeys the physics.
- **Reservoir computing (ESN)**: uses a random recurrent network to forecast chaotic systems efficiently; a classic tool for chaotic time-series prediction.

## 16.6 Use cases

- **Weather and climate**: fundamentally fluid dynamics, combining numerical weather prediction with data-driven models.
- **Epidemics**: SIR-type transmission models are dynamical systems used to forecast infection curves.
- **Engineering and control**: mechanical vibration, circuits, robots — clear state evolution, well suited to dynamical-systems modelling.
- **Physiological signals**: certain dynamical features of heart rhythm and EEG can be used for diagnosis.

## 16.7 Practical tips

- **When to use this perspective**: when there is a clear underlying mechanism, when you want long-term / structural understanding, or when you need to inject physical constraints into the model. Purely data-driven short-term forecasting may not need it.
- **Accept chaos for what it is**: estimate the Lyapunov exponent first to judge how far the "predictability horizon" reaches, and don't insist on accuracy beyond that limit.
- **Connections to earlier lectures**: sliding window = lag embedding; KUN's multiple scales = dynamics at different time scales — dynamical systems give these engineering practices a unified theoretical account.
