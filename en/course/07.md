## 7.1 Why autocorrelation is central

Ordinary machine learning assumes samples are independent of each other; time series are exactly the opposite — **today is highly correlated with yesterday**, and it is precisely this "correlation of a series with its own past" that makes forecasting possible. If a series bore no relation to its past, it would be pure noise, and nobody could forecast it. So **quantifying this relationship** is the most basic and most useful tool in time series analysis: it tells us "how far back the series remembers", and directly guides which model to choose and of what order.

## 7.2 The autocorrelation function (ACF)

The **autocorrelation function (ACF)** measures the correlation between $x_t$ and $x_{t-k}$, lagged by $k$ steps:

$$\rho(k) = \frac{\text{Cov}(x_t,\, x_{t-k})}{\text{Var}(x_t)} = \frac{\gamma(k)}{\gamma(0)}.$$

An ACF plot has the lag $k$ on the horizontal axis and $\rho(k)$ on the vertical axis, with values in $[-1, 1]$. It tells you:

- How far back the series "remembers" — if $\rho(k)$ decays quickly, its memory is short.
- Whether there is a period — if $\rho(k)$ rises again at certain $k$ (such as 12, 24), that is a periodic signal.

## 7.3 The partial autocorrelation function (PACF)

The ACF has a problem: the correlation between $x_t$ and $x_{t-2}$ may arise only because both are correlated with $x_{t-1}$ — an indirect relationship "passed along" the chain. **Partial autocorrelation (PACF)** removes the influence of the intermediate steps and looks only at the **direct relationship** between $x_t$ and $x_{t-k}$.

This distinction is very useful, because AR and MA models leave different "fingerprints" on the two plots.

## 7.4 Identifying models from the ACF / PACF

This is the core plot-reading technique of the classical Box-Jenkins method:

| Model | ACF | PACF |
|------|-----|------|
| AR(p) | Tails off (exponential / oscillating decay) | **Cuts off** after lag $p$ |
| MA(q) | **Cuts off** after lag $q$ | Tails off |
| ARMA(p,q) | Tails off | Tails off |

"Cuts off" = suddenly drops inside the confidence band; "tails off" = decays slowly. The intuition: **wherever the PACF cuts off sets the AR order $p$; wherever the ACF cuts off sets the MA order $q$**. Lecture 8 uses this table directly when choosing ARIMA orders.

## 7.5 White noise: the yardstick of whether a model is good enough

If a model's **residuals are white noise** (the ACF lies within the confidence interval at every lag), then all the "predictable relationships" in the series have been absorbed by the model — which is exactly the end point we want.

- **White noise**: a purely random sequence with zero mean, constant variance and zero autocorrelation at every lag.
- **Ljung-Box test**: tests in one go whether the autocorrelations at the first several lags are jointly zero; it is the standard tool for diagnosing residuals.

## 7.6 The frequency-domain view: the periodogram

The "frequency-domain dual" of the ACF is the **power spectral density (PSD)**:

- The ACF looks at correlation along the **time dimension**;
- The PSD looks at the distribution of energy along the **frequency dimension**, with peaks corresponding to the dominant periods (12 months, 7 days).

The two views complement each other — sometimes a period stands out more clearly in the spectrum than in the ACF.

## 7.7 Practical tips

- **Make it stationary before looking at the ACF**: the ACF of a non-stationary series decays slowly and linearly; that is an illusion caused by the trend, not a genuine correlation structure. Apply the differencing of Lecture 6 first.
- **The plots won't be clean**: the ACF/PACF of real data is often ambiguous; reading the plots only gives candidates, and the final order must be decided together with AIC / BIC (Lecture 8).
- **Use the residual ACF for acceptance**: after building a model, always go back and check whether the residual ACF looks like white noise.
