### Those little stars beside Jupiter

In January 1610, Galileo Galilei swept the night sky with a telescope he had improved himself, and next to Jupiter he saw three or four small points of light strung out in a line. That in itself was not remarkable — but the next night their relative positions had changed; the third night they changed again. Someone else might have glanced and moved on, but Galileo did one crucial thing: **he drew their positions night after night**, dated each drawing, and kept it up for several weeks.

### The difficulty: what exactly are these dots?

The difficulty was this: looking at any single night, you simply could not say what these little stars were — background stars that happened to be there, or something else? Answering that relied not on one night's “snapshot” but on **linking the nightly positions into a series** and looking for regularity in it.

### The solution: let the nightly series of positions speak for itself

Lay the sketches of successive nights side by side, and the pattern emerges: the four little stars never strayed far from Jupiter, swinging back and forth on either side of it, none of them ever wandering off. From this Galileo concluded that they were **satellites orbiting Jupiter** — later called the Galilean moons (Jupiter I to IV: Io, Europa, Ganymede, Callisto). This was strong circumstantial evidence for Copernican heliocentrism: not every celestial body revolves around the Earth after all. He published the discovery in his 1610 *Sidereus Nuncius* (*Starry Messenger*), which caused a sensation across Europe.

Better still was what followed: each of the four moons has a stable orbital period (the innermost, Io, takes about 1.77 days per orbit), and their positions and mutual eclipses can be forecast with clockwork precision. Galileo therefore proposed a bold application — use the eclipses of Jupiter's moons as a “**clock in the sky**”, so that ships at sea could read off the local time against an ephemeris and thus compute their **longitude**. On a pitching deck the scheme never became practical, but on land it really did help correct maps. In 1676, Rømer, by measuring how the eclipses of Io came **sometimes earlier, sometimes later** than forecast, made the first estimate of the speed of light — once again, the “residuals” of a series were hiding something real.

### What it means for time series today

This is one of the earliest **quantitative astronomical time series** of the telescope age: record night by night → discover a pattern → build a model (the moons' orbits) → forecast the future (ephemerides), with real applications in navigation and timekeeping. It belongs to the same era and the same spirit as [Kepler chasing the residuals of Mars](../kepler-mars/) — **a single observation tells you nothing; linked into a series, it acquires structure**. And Rømer reading the speed of light from the timing deviations of eclipses confirmed once more that “residuals are not noise; they may be signal”.
