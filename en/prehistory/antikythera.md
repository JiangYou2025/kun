### A bronze lump from a shipwreck

In 1901, a group of sponge divers off the Greek island of Antikythera found, on the seabed, a Roman shipwreck from the 1st century BC. They brought up bronze statues and glassware — and an utterly unremarkable lump of corroded, fused bronze. It lay in a museum for the better part of a century, and no one could make sense of it. Only in the late 20th century, and above all in 2005, when the Antikythera Mechanism Research Project used high-resolution X-ray tomography to see through the corrosion, could people finally read the tooth counts of the gears inside and the thousands of Greek characters engraved on its surfaces — and at last see what it really was.

It was built around **150–100 BC** in the Hellenistic world. That era already held two key things: [the eclipse-cycle data the Babylonians had accumulated over centuries](../babylon-diaries/), and the geometric models of astronomy developed by the Greeks.

### The difficulty: turning cycles into a physical tool

The difficulty was this: the various periods of the heavenly motions were already roughly known, yet every time you wanted to look up the sky or work out an eclipse, you had to redo a tedious calculation. Could these cycles be “**materialized**” into a device you could consult at any time, so that prediction became a mechanical action instead of a computation started from scratch each time?

### The solution: simulating the sky with gear ratios

The answer was a device of astonishing precision: more than thirty intermeshing bronze gears (originally perhaps 37 or more), each with a carefully chosen number of teeth, using ratios of tooth counts to approximate astronomical periods. It used the **19-year Metonic cycle** to reconcile the lunar and solar calendars; it used the **223-month Saros cycle** to drive a spiral dial that forecast eclipses, even marking the approximate hour and colour of each eclipse; it even had an Olympiad dial marking the years of the ancient games held every four years. More ingenious still, it used a pair of eccentrically meshed gears to simulate the Moon's uneven motion, “sometimes fast, sometimes slow” — the mechanical embodiment of Hipparchus's lunar theory.

To use it, you simply turned the crank on its side to bring the pointer to a given date, and the dials on the front and back would rotate in step, showing the positions of the Sun, Moon and planets at that time and forecasting the next eclipse. It was the work of anonymous Greek craftsmen and astronomers, and the complexity of its gearwork **remained unmatched for more than a thousand years** — equalled again only by the mechanical astronomical clocks of medieval Europe.

### What it means for time series today

It turned “periodic prediction” from a paper calculation into **mechanical automation**: known regularities were frozen into the tooth ratios of gears, reducing a prediction to a turn of the crank. In a sense it is the distant ancestor of the later [tide-predicting machine](../), and of every automatic forecasting system today — the idea of packaging a “model” into a device, so that the user only enters a time and reads off the future, had already taken shape two thousand years ago.
