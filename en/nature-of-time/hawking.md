> This is one of the stories in [the nature of time](../).

Once the Big Bang theory was established, it left behind an awkward singularity: at $t=0$, density and curvature diverge and the laws of physics break down. “Time has a beginning” is both alluring and troublesome — what came “before” the beginning? In the 1970s and 1980s, Hawking tried to sidestep this singularity using ideas from quantum gravity.

With Hartle he put forward the **no-boundary proposal**, whose key tool is **imaginary time**: in the calculation, time is multiplied by $i$, making it mathematically behave like one more direction of space. Time and space then merge in the very early universe, and the universe becomes a smooth geometry **with no boundary and no starting point** — just as the Earth's surface is finite yet has no edge, so that “north of the North Pole” is a meaningless question, “before the Big Bang” is equally meaningless. He also showed that black holes slowly evaporate through quantum effects (Hawking radiation), and put all of this into his worldwide bestseller *A Brief History of Time*.

The no-boundary proposal has not been confirmed to this day, yet it is one of the most elegant attempts to answer the question of “the beginning of time”: it does away with the singularity, and with the very question of “before”. Together with Hawking radiation, it stitches gravity, quantum theory and thermodynamics together in a rare way, and has deeply influenced how cosmology asks its questions.

Hawking reminds us that some questions about time have no answer because they **presuppose a framework that does not exist**. We habitually assume a “before the start” for our data, but at certain scales the question itself may simply need to be dropped.
