## 9.1 Why turn to deep learning

Classical models (Lecture 8) rely on a human to choose the order, apply differencing and judge the linear structure. When data is **plentiful and complex** — thousands of related series, strong nonlinearity, long-range dependencies — that manual approach struggles. The motivation for deep learning is to **let the network itself learn from the raw sequence which features to extract and how far back to remember**, doing "feature engineering + modelling" together, end to end. The price is more data and more compute.

## 9.2 RNN: born for sequences

A **recurrent neural network (RNN)** maintains a **hidden state** $h_t$ at every time step, compressing "the information seen so far" into it:

$$h_t = \tanh(W_h h_{t-1} + W_x x_t + b).$$

Each step "remembers" what came before, a natural fit for sequences. But it has a fatal flaw — **vanishing gradients**: after backpropagating through too many time steps, the gradient decays exponentially, so **long-term dependencies cannot be learned** (information from a few dozen steps back is essentially lost).

## 9.3 LSTM: remembering long-term information with "gates"

**Long short-term memory (LSTM, 1997)** was designed specifically to solve the vanishing-gradient problem. It uses three **gates** plus a **cell state** to finely control the flow of information:

- **Forget gate**: decides which old information to discard.
- **Input gate**: decides which new information to write.
- **Cell state**: an "information highway" that lets important information pass through the whole sequence with almost no decay.

Thanks to this controllable memory, LSTM became the standard for deep learning on time series before the Transformer arrived.

## 9.4 GRU: a lighter LSTM

The **gated recurrent unit (GRU)** is a simplified LSTM that merges the three gates into two (a reset gate + an update gate). With fewer parameters and faster training, it performs close to LSTM on most tasks — a good choice when data or compute is limited.

## 9.5 TCN: replacing recurrence with convolution

The **temporal convolutional network (TCN)** takes a different path: instead of stepping through recurrently, it processes the sequence with **causal convolutions** (looking only at the past, never peeking at the future). Its strengths come from three design choices:

- **Dilated convolution**: skips double layer by layer, covering a very long receptive field with very few layers.
- **Residual connections**: keep deep networks stable and trainable.
- **Parallel computation**: the whole sequence is computed in one go, **much faster** than an RNN advancing step by step, and with no vanishing gradients.

## 9.6 Seq2Seq and attention: the road to the Transformer

**Encoder-decoder (Seq2Seq)**: the encoder compresses the input sequence into a vector, and the decoder generates the output sequence step by step from it. But squeezing the entire history into one vector is a bottleneck. The **attention mechanism** lets the decoder "look back" at all of the encoder's hidden states at every step, focusing on the most relevant time steps as needed. This idea led directly to the Transformer of Lecture 10.

## 9.7 When to use deep learning

Deep models are not a panacea; weigh things up before using them:

- **Data volume**: neural networks are "data-hungry". With only a few hundred points, ARIMA / ETS are often more stable and more accurate.
- **Sharing across series**: training thousands of related series together (a global model) is the setting where deep learning has its biggest advantage over classical methods.
- **Costs**: slow training, hyperparameter tuning, poor interpretability, prone to overfitting — so **always compare against the linear baseline of Lecture 3** to prove the complexity buys a real improvement.
