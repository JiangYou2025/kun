> This is one of the stories in [the nature of time](../).

In the Hindu and Buddhist view of the cosmos, time is a vast cycle. Measured in **kalpas**, the universe goes through creation, preservation, destruction and rebirth, over and over again; living beings, meanwhile, wander without rest through the round of **saṃsāra**. This stands in direct opposition to the one-time, linear time of “creation to the end of days” in the later Hebrew–Christian tradition. For ancient agricultural civilizations that witnessed, day after day and year after year, the return of the seasons and the stars, cyclic time was the most natural of intuitions.

Cyclic time is not measured with clocks but “told” through myth and calendar: observable cycles — day and night, the phases of the moon, the four seasons — are magnified into great cycles on a cosmic scale and then given ethical meaning and a meaning of liberation. Since everything will come round again, **liberation** is not reaching some end point but **stepping out of the cycle itself**.

This idea shaped a thousand years of South Asian philosophy and spiritual practice, and it has left echoes in modern times: Nietzsche's “eternal recurrence” and the cosmological conjecture of an “oscillating universe” are both variants of cyclic time. Its deepest reminder is that “time has a direction” is not a self-evident truth but **the choice of a particular culture**.

When we lay out data along a one-way time axis, we have already quietly taken the side of linear time. Yet seasonality, periods and shapes that keep returning sneak the “cycle” back in — perhaps linear and cyclic are simply two ways of reading the same time.
