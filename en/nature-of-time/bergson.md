> This is one of the stories in [the nature of time](../).

By the end of the 19th century, science had completely spatialized time: physics drew it as a number line $t$ that could be marked off at will. Bergson was dissatisfied — he felt this straight line had lost the most important thing about time: the continuous, indivisible experience we **live within**.

He proposed **duration (la durée)**: time as truly experienced is a flowing, interpenetrating whole, the past folding layer upon layer into the present, and it cannot be cut into equal segments the way space can. His method was **intuition** rather than analysis — the moment you line time up and chop it into cells, what you are handling is no longer time but its **spatialized shadow**. Clocks measure the latter; life lives the former.

Duration deeply influenced 20th-century literature (Proust), phenomenology and process philosophy. It also led to a famous public clash with Einstein: in 1922 the two confronted each other face to face, Bergson insisting that “lived time” cannot be exhausted by physical time. Physics won the scientific battlefield, but the dimension of “lived time” could never again be erased.

Bergson is perhaps the sharpest reminder of what “time series” actually do: when we cut continuous experience into evenly spaced sample points, we gain computability but lose duration. Every cell on the horizontal axis is the same length — yet no two stretches of lived time are ever truly the same length.
